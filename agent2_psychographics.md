# Agent 2 — Psychographics & Customer Behaviour
## Research input for redesigning an Indian restaurant A4 menu card

**Prepared:** November 2026 · **Scope:** research only, no design
**Boundaries:** eye-path and layout mechanics are covered in `agent4_layout.md`; colour/typography in `agent5_color_typography.md`; Indian cultural and regulatory conventions (veg/non-veg marks, thali pricing, spice icons) in `agent6_indian_context.md`. This file covers *who the guest is and how they decide*, and only names a design lever where the behavioural evidence is specific to that customer type.

---

## How to read this report

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[EVIDENCE]** | Peer-reviewed experiment, field study, or meta-analysis. Direction is supported; effect size may not transfer to another country, format or price point. |
| **[INDUSTRY]** | Trade press, platform data, consultant or operator consensus, vendor surveys. Directional only. |
| **[WEAK]** | Small, non-representative, self-reported or unreplicated. Do not build a rule on it alone. |
| **[DATA MISSING]** | No usable evidence found. Say so; do not fill the gap with folklore. |

Three standing cautions:

1. **Almost no menu-behaviour research has been run on Indian dine-in customers.** The strongest experiments are from the US (Cornell, Penn State, Duke, Illinois) and from Korea, Taiwan and Europe. Indian data in this report is platform data, trade surveys or small local studies. Treat foreign effect sizes as *hypotheses to test*, not constants.
2. **Menu-psychology content online is contaminated by folklore** — the "golden triangle", "$ signs cost you 8%", "the 27% descriptive-label lift" and "long menus paralyse diners" are repeated far more often than they are replicated. Where a claim survives here, the primary study exists and I say what it actually found.
3. **Personality is a weak predictor compared with occasion.** The same guest reads the same card differently on a Tuesday lunch and a birthday dinner. Segment the *moment* as well as the *person*.

## 1. Customer personalities and dining motivations

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] Dining out runs on two independent value systems.** *Utilitarian* value is nourishment, speed, convenience, price and "I need to eat". *Hedonic* value is pleasure, novelty, atmosphere, social occasion and treat. Both independently predict satisfaction and revisit intention; neither cancels the other. The construct runs from Batra & Ahtola (1991) through restaurant applications (Ryu, Han & Jang 2010, fast-casual; Ha & Jang 2010, ethnic restaurants).
- **[EVIDENCE] Format sets the default weighting.** In fast-casual, utilitarian value outweighs hedonic value in driving satisfaction and behavioural intention (Ryu et al. 2010, US university sample). In full-service and ethnic dining, hedonic value carries more weight for guests who are *unfamiliar* with the cuisine (Ha & Jang 2010, 607 American customers of Korean restaurants).
- **[EVIDENCE] Familiarity flips the weighting.** For low-familiarity guests, hedonic value drove positive behavioural intentions more strongly; for high-familiarity guests, utilitarian value dominated (Ha & Jang 2010). This is the single most useful finding in this section: *the same card must carry both signals, and which one should lead depends on how familiar the food is to that guest.*
- **[EVIDENCE] Involvement, not demographics, separates the two modes.** A 559-respondent US casual-dining study found three involvement segments: low-involvement diners were pushed out by utilitarian reasons (can't cook, convenience) and pulled by location and speed; high-involvement diners were pushed by celebration and socialising and pulled by atmosphere and social facilitators such as happy hour (Josiam et al. 2015, *Hospitality Review*). Low involvement is not the same as low spend — it is a different reading task.
- **[EVIDENCE] Occasion changes the mode inside the same person.** Parents surveyed about eating out with children chose "enjoyable" and "as a treat" far more often than "ease/convenience", although the same parents said health drove their at-home food choices (Brindal et al. 2020, n=1,802 Australian parents). People switch modes by context.
- **[EVIDENCE] Measurable traits that predict menu reading** (not exhaustive): food involvement, food neophobia (Pliner & Hobden 1992), variety-seeking (VARSEEK), price consciousness, price–quality schema, and impulsiveness. Neophobia scores correlate with trait anxiety and with the Experience-Seeking subscale of sensation seeking, and are unrelated to gender (Pliner & Hobden 1992). Extraversion predicted impulse ordering in a restaurant survey; openness did not (Taiwanese restaurant impulse study, see §8).
- **[INDUSTRY] Indian framing matches the two-motive model.** RedSeer's 2025 food-services study of metro/Tier-1 consumers reports the main drivers of increased outside meals as celebrations and social occasions (45% agreeing they prefer non-home food to spend time with friends and family) and variety (30%). That is a hedonic-led pattern in urban India, and it sits beside a strong utilitarian value check (see §3).

### Evidence / source summary

One sobering result from the same involvement study: service quality was the strongest predictor of diner loyalty (Beta = .253), ahead of any menu attribute. A menu supports loyalty; it does not create it.

The hedonic–utilitarian split is one of the better-replicated ideas in hospitality research, but almost all of it is cross-sectional survey work with self-reported value scales. Ha & Jang's familiarity moderation is the most decision-relevant result and comes from one cuisine and one national sample. The involvement segmentation is a convenience sample at a single US university, so treat segment sizes as illustrative, not population estimates.

### Practical recommendation

Serve both readers within one glance per section:

- **Utilitarian signals:** price legibility, portion or "serves" information, clear veg/non-veg marks, plain-language description of what the dish is, complete-meal bundles (thali, meal for two), and no ambiguity about taxes.
- **Hedonic signals:** one short sensory or origin line per dish, a small number of evocative section names, and one dish per section that reads as an occasion ("for the table", "weekend only").
- Do not try to convert a utilitarian reader into a hedonic one with adjectives. The evidence says both motives coexist; the card's job is to serve both.

### Confidence

**High** that the two-motive model is real and useful. **Medium** that the specific weighting transfers to Indian casual dining.

### Limitations

Self-report and common-method bias; "hedonic" is measured with different scales across papers, so effect sizes are not comparable; few studies observe actual choice; no Indian replication found.

## 2. Variety seekers vs familiarity seekers

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] Variety-seeking and food neophobia are stable, measurable traits.** High VARSEEK consumers seek stimulation and switch dishes; high-neophobia consumers avoid unfamiliar food and prefer the known. They are negatively correlated but not opposites — a person can be adventurous in one cuisine and conservative in another.
- **[EVIDENCE] Familiarity is a risk reducer, not just a preference.** Adding familiar flavours, appearance, presentation and preparation methods to a novel dish reduced the barrier to trying ethnic food for US consumers (Hwang & Lin 2010, Asian menu items). In a 601-person Polish study using a Vietnamese menu, high-neophobia respondents were significantly less likely to choose dishes with unfamiliar ingredients, and the share choosing *nothing at all* in a course was highest in that group (24.5% for starters).
- **[EVIDENCE] Neophobia is not simply age.** Age effects are mixed across studies; what repeats is that trait neophobia predicts avoidance and that prior exposure predicts acceptance.
- **[EVIDENCE] Variety-seeking rises in groups.** Real tables order more varied dishes than randomly assembled "nominal" tables of the same size (Ariely & Levav 2000; 814 lunch slips, 2,202 diners). The effect comes from the social act of ordering, not from individual preferences (see §7).
- **[EVIDENCE, against the folklore] Long menus do not reliably paralyse diners.** The popular claim rests on the jam study. A meta-analysis of 50 studies found the average choice-overload effect is essentially zero (Scheibehenne et al. 2010); a later meta-analysis found it only under specific moderators — high assortment complexity, low preference certainty, and a decision goal that rewards comparison (Chernev et al. 2015). A hungry, committed diner is close to the opposite of those conditions.
- **[DATA MISSING] No credible evidence for an optimal number of dishes per section.** Cut dishes for readability, stock and execution — honest operational reasons — not because psychology demands it.
- **[INDUSTRY] Indian urban diners are experimenting more, but from a familiar base.** Swiggy's *How India Eats 2025* (with Kearney) reports a 20% rise in unique cuisines ordered per customer and a 30% rise in restaurants explored, while familiar North Indian and Italian cuisines show the strongest growth at lower price points. Both novelty and familiarity are growing at once.

### Evidence / source summary

Trait evidence is solid (FNS is a 30-year instrument with behavioural validation). Menu-length folklore is contradicted by meta-analysis. The practical tactics below are inference from trait evidence, not tested menu interventions.

### Practical recommendation

- **Anchor each section with 2–3 unmistakable dishes** — the ones people came for. A familiar seeker should be able to order without reading a paragraph.
- **Place 1–2 exploratory dishes per section**, ideally where a reader who is still browsing will meet them without forcing a conservative diner past them. (Position effects are real; see §7 and `agent4_layout.md`.)
- **Language:** let a known dish keep its name. Attach novelty as one distinguishing clause — "smoked", "black garlic", "Kashmiri chilli", "cooked in copper handi" — rather than re-branding butter chicken into an unrecognisable English construction.
- **Do not hide the familiar dish** to make the menu look modern. For a high-neophobia guest, an all-unfamiliar section produces "I'll have the safest thing" or no order.

### Confidence

**High** that the traits exist and predict avoidance/exploration. **Medium** that the placement tactics move mix. **Low** for any specific dish-count rule.

### Limitations

Most neophobia studies use mocked menus with no prices, no service and no social pressure; real menus carry price risk and group negotiation, which amplify conservatism.

## 3. Value-conscious customers

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] Price sensitivity is a cluster of beliefs, not one trait.** Bargain shopping, price knowledge and price–quality beliefs predicted restaurant price sensitivity in a 106-person US survey; price-sensitive consumers were also value-conscious (McCall & Bruneau 2010, *Journal of Foodservice Business Research*). Value consciousness means "what do I get for this money", which is not the same as "cheapest".
- **[EVIDENCE] Scarcity changes what the eye does.** Participants given a $20 budget (vs $100) spent a larger share of menu dwell time on prices (21.1% vs 15.2%), less on food items (35.1% vs 52.0%), and recalled prices more accurately — but *missed a discount clause* at the bottom of the menu (Tomm & Zhao 2016, eye-tracking, n≈190). Attention narrows to the number and away from the rest.
- **[EVIDENCE] Price-conscious guests have different scanpaths.** A study comparing traditional Chinese and regular menus found that "price-conscious" consumers did not follow the general scanpath, and that layout changed how menu information was processed (Ngan et al. 2022, *Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research*). There is no single reading pattern to design for.
- **[EVIDENCE] Price is often checked late, not first.** In a lab-in-the-field lunch study, 66% of participants looked at prices *last* before ordering, and 74% viewed the price of the dish they eventually chose (ESRI 2019 calorie-placement experiment). Price is used to verify a shortlist as much as to build one.
- **[EVIDENCE] What is looked at longest is the dish name, then price, then image.** A Korean eye-tracking study (single study; treat as directional) found menu names drew the longest fixation, followed by price and image; calorie information drew little attention. Gender differences appeared (men dwelt on price longer; women on names and special marks).
- **[EVIDENCE] Currency cues can reduce spend — modestly, in one setting.** At a Culinary Institute of America lunch restaurant, parties given numeral-only prices spent $5.55 more per check than parties given "$20.00" or "twenty dollars" — about 8.15% more per person. Party size, dwell time and willingness to spend explained far more of the variance than price typography (Yang, Kimes & Sessarego 2009, *IJHM*). This is one restaurant, one meal period, US, 2009.
- **[EVIDENCE] Price endings carry meaning, but only relative to neighbours.** Nine-ending prices are associated with *value* and zero-ending prices with *quality* (Naipaul & Parsa 2001; Schindler, Parsa & Naipaul 2011). A French pizzeria field study (1,271 customers) found a 7.99 € pizza was chosen far more often than the same pizza at 8.00 € — 49.5% vs 34.2% of pizza orders — **but only when the other items ended in 00**. When every price ended in 9, the target item lost its advantage (Guéguen et al. 2009). The "9" is a contrast effect, not a magic digit.
- **[EVIDENCE] There are distinct value segments.** A 613-person US survey clustered restaurant consumers into sensory-oriented, taste-oriented organic, local/natural, quantity-oriented and quality-oriented segments. Quality-oriented consumers spent the most per meal and were least likely to visit fast food; quantity-oriented consumers spent least and visited fast food most (2023, *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Insights*). "Value" means different things to each.
- **[INDUSTRY] Value is now a top-three menu trend, framed as abundance not cheapness.** The National Restaurant Association's 2026 *What's Hot* forecast (operator/chef survey, not consumers) ranks value menus and options third, behind local sourcing and comfort food, and pairs it with "clear menu labelling, icons and cues" at fifth. Indian trade framing is similar: perceived abundance and consistency, not the lowest price.
- **[INDUSTRY] Indian price sensitivity is currently elevated.** A LocalCircles poll of 19,328 respondents (self-selected, not representative) found 57% reporting restaurant price increases in the past week; a separate 2026 poll found three in four food-app users unhappy about listed prices and taxes. Use as a signal of mood, not a statistic about your guests.
- **[DATA MISSING] No Indian research on acceptable price bands, price thresholds, or the effect of "99" endings in rupees.** Any specific recommendation about ₹299 vs ₹300 is extrapolation.

### Evidence / source summary

Strong evidence that price draws attention and that price presentation can shift spend slightly. Weak evidence that any one format is universally better. The numeral-only finding is the most cited and the most over-generalised: it is a single upscale-casual lunch study, and the authors themselves say operational factors dominate.

### Practical recommendation

- **Make price the easiest thing to find after the dish name.** Align prices in one column; do not bury them mid-description. A value-conscious guest should not have to hunt, and a hedonic guest should not be confronted by repeated currency symbols.
- **Choose one price-ending system per card** and keep it. Mixed 9s and 0s make the 9s look like bargains and the 0s look expensive (or vice versa). For a mid-market Indian restaurant, round numbers read as more confident and are easier to add mentally; 9-endings signal value and belong in the value section, if anywhere.
- **State taxes and service charge once, clearly.** Surprise at the bill destroys the value perception the menu worked to build.
- **Show what the money buys:** "serves 2", number of pieces, thali composition, "unlimited rice", add-on prices. Quantity-oriented guests scan for this; quality-oriented guests scan for provenance.
- **Give a genuine entry point** in each section. A value guest who cannot find one affordable dish stops reading the section, not just the dish.
- **Avoid discount language in the premium sections.** It re-frames the whole card as a deal.

### Confidence

**High** that price is heavily attended and that surprise charges damage value perception. **Medium** that numeral-only pricing raises spend in an Indian context. **Low** on specific price endings and thresholds.

### Limitations

Field studies are single-site and old; eye-tracking samples are small and lab-based; Indian price psychology is unstudied; self-selected polls overstate distress.

## 4. Premium and luxury customers

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] Price is read as a quality signal, with a cost.** In a menu experiment, higher-priced restaurant versions were judged higher quality but *worse value* and were less likely to be tried (2005 menu experiment; odd pricing had no effect on any measure). Premium pricing therefore needs supporting cues, or it suppresses trial.
- **[EVIDENCE] Description complexity raises perceived quality and expected price.** More complex menu descriptions increased perceived quality, purchase likelihood and the price respondents expected to pay; the presence of a price anchor mattered less (2008, *Journal of Foodservice Business Research*, 160 US students). The effect is about *specificity and preparation detail*, not long words.
- **[EVIDENCE] Provenance raises willingness to pay, strongest at the top.** A choice-based conjoint study of 1,200+ US consumers found positive WTP for local sourcing across casual, fast-casual and fine dining, with fine-dining customers showing the highest WTP per additional level of local sourcing (University of Kentucky thesis, stated preference). A separate stated-choice study (n=328) found local independent ownership, local sourcing and food-quality signals all raised choice and WTP; a food-quality signal in a local independent restaurant was associated with a 40% WTP premium over a chain (Penn State, 2020).
- **[EVIDENCE] Naming style changes what people expect and will pay.** In a 300-person between-subjects experiment, an "ancestral" name (heritage/terroir framing) raised expectations, post-tasting liking, perceived authenticity and WTP versus a traditional name; a "gourmet" name raised WTP only. Effects were stronger for high-neophilia guests and for guests with higher gastronomic cultural capital (Valdés-Alarcón et al. 2026, *Journal of Sensory Studies*). This is the best current evidence for premium menu language.
- **[EVIDENCE] Unjustified adjectives backfire.** Wansink's own caution: descriptive labels raise expectations, and if the plate does not meet them, the result is negative (2001). Over-claiming is a reversible gain, then a permanent loss.
- **[WEAK] Premium guests still punish insufficient value.** A text-analysis thesis of Yelp reviews found value/portion dissatisfaction rose with price level and that expensive restaurants were less tolerant of disconfirmation. Single thesis, observational, US; direction only.
- **[INDUSTRY] Indian premium is experience-led.** KPMG's India CX Report 2025 (QSR-focused) reports 30% of customers saying consistency of high-quality food and service justifies a higher price, 29% willing to pay a premium for new flavours, and 30% for transparency. It is a QSR sample, not fine dining, but it shows the premium justifications Indians name: consistency, novelty, honesty.

### Evidence / source summary

The direction is consistent: specificity, provenance and craft language raise expected quality and WTP; generic luxury adjectives do not. Magnitudes come from stated-preference and lab experiments, often with students or online panels. No Indian fine-dining menu experiment was found.

### Practical recommendation

- **Justify with verifiable specifics, not mood adjectives.** "Aged 24 months", "Kashmiri chilli", "stone-ground masala", "cooked on order in a copper handi", "sourced from X farm" beats "exquisite", "authentic", "royal".
- **Give every premium section one signature dish with a 6–10 word provenance clause.** The rest can be shorter. Uniform long descriptions raise reading cost and flatten the hierarchy.
- **Keep premium prices as round numbers**, in a clean column, with no strikethroughs, no "was/now", no combo pressure.
- **Use one elevated but readable name per section**, not a full menu of invented names. Ancestral framing is powerful but only where the claim is true; a fabricated heritage claim is the fastest way to lose a premium guest.
- **Signal craft in the structure as well as the words:** a separate "signatures" or "chef's table" block is a legitimate premium cue; a discount block next to it is not.

### Confidence

**Medium–High** that specificity and provenance raise perceived quality and WTP. **Medium** on the size of the premium in India. **Low** on any exact price premium to charge.

### Limitations

Most WTP evidence is hypothetical (stated choice), not observed payment; student and online samples; Western cuisines; "luxury" is not a single segment; Indian guests may weight hospitality and abundance more than provenance.

## 5. Families: decision dynamics, kids' influence, sharing

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] The family is a negotiating unit, and the mother is often the gatekeeper.** In a Taiwanese casual-dining study, mothers played the dominant role in the family dining-out decision; children's influence was similar to fathers' for restaurant type, specific restaurant and food ordering, and teenage children had more influence than fathers (2016, *Journal of Hospitality Marketing & Management*). A southern-India discrete-choice study of 68 families found a two-step process: each spouse forms an individual utility, then the couple negotiates a joint choice; bargaining power was more equal where both spouses earned (Cornell Hospitality Quarterly, 2013).
- **[EVIDENCE] Children decide their own order more often than parents think.** In the same Australian survey of 1,802 parents (Brindal et al. 2020), child taste preferences drove 85.9% of parents' actual item choices, ahead of nutrition (37.3%) and "something new to try" (4.5%) — parents say health, but they order for the child's palate. In a US quick-service study of 218 parent–child pairs, 80% of children selected their meal without parental involvement and 63% had decided before entering; parents' involvement did not make the meal healthier (2020, *Nutrients*). In a San Diego table-service observation of 102 parties and 150 children, parents placed 62% of orders, and parental involvement fell with the child's age.
- **[EVIDENCE] Children's menu use is high; the kids' menu is a real decision point.** 63% of children ordered from the children's menu in a US study, and half of parents said the child determined their own order; taste was the most common reason for the choice (2017, *JNE&B*). The same study found only 8% of those children ordered a healthier kids' meal.
- **[EVIDENCE] Sharing is normal, and it changes what gets ordered.** 34% of children shared their meal in the San Diego observation, and **100% of sharing children had ordered from the adult menu**, versus 69% of non-sharers. A child who shares is effectively ordering adult food — a value and variety argument for portion and platter design.
- **[EVIDENCE] What children say would make them try a new kids' item:** pictures (44%), descriptions (25%), server suggestions (22%), nutrition information (19%), price/value (15%) (same US study, self-report). Pictures lead, but this is stated preference, not sales.
- **[WEAK] Indian family data points in the same direction but is thin.** A 2026 descriptive survey of 108 families in Palwal/Hodal, Haryana, reported that 82% of parents consider children's preferences when ordering, 39.8% say children have moderate influence, 67.6% order separate meals for children, and 49% dine out less than monthly. Advertising exposure (61%), peers (59%) and menu variety (34%) were named as influences. Small n, descriptive statistics only, local journal — use as a hypothesis, not a benchmark.
- **[INDUSTRY / CULTURAL] Indian sharing is routine and priced flexibly.** "One by two" and half portions are normal requests, and restaurants often accommodate them even at a margin cost (Times of India, Pune, 2025). A thali is an individual complete meal, but sides, breads and rice are shared. No controlled study measures how menu structure affects Indian family sharing — **[DATA MISSING]**.

### Evidence / source summary

Child influence is well established across countries. The strongest designs observe real orders (San Diego; QSR study). The Indian Haryana study is the only India-specific family-menu data found and is methodologically weak. Couples' joint decision-making has one solid Indian study.

### Practical recommendation

- **Build a family block, not a kids' ghetto.** 2–4 items, plainly named, with mild/spice indication, a picture on at most one or two, and a portion that can be shared. A 12-item kids' menu increases reading cost for the parent and invites negotiation.
- **Show "serves 2 / serves 4" and half portions** on breads, rice, curries and platters. This serves the sharing family, the value guest and the group, and reduces the "one by two" conversation at the table.
- **Keep the children's items inside the main card**, not on a separate insert. The child is choosing, and the parent is checking price and spice at the same time.
- **Mark spice with words as well as icons** ("mild", "medium", "hot"), because the person reading may be a child or a grandparent, and chilli icons are not universally understood.
- **Price family bundles so the arithmetic is easy** (thali, meal for two, meal for four). Families calculate total spend, not per-dish value.
- **Do not hide the familiar.** Children and grandparents are the most neophobic guests at the table; a family section of unrecognisable dishes fails twice.

### Confidence

**High** that children influence the order and that sharing is common. **Medium** on the magnitude in Indian households. **Low** on kids' menu design effects — the evidence is self-report.

### Limitations

US observational studies; Indian study is descriptive and small; children's stated preferences diverge from behaviour; no data on multi-generational Indian tables or on price sensitivity by family income.

## 6. Young customers: novelty, exploration, shareability

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] Novelty-seeking is a trait, not an age.** Food neophilia and variety-seeking are measurable traits, and studies disagree on whether age predicts them; what repeats is that the trait, not the birthday, predicts exploration (see §3). Treat "young = adventurous" as a tendency, not a rule — and assume every age group contains both.
- **[EVIDENCE] Visual and experiential cues do shift choice.** An appetising photo of one vegetarian dish increased stated ordering of that dish, and the mechanism was anticipatory enjoyment triggered by imagining eating it (2023, *Journal of Sustainable Tourism*, 742 respondents, stated choice). In a nine-week café field experiment (~6,500 transactions), real photographs produced higher sales shares than no image or an illustration for two of three featured dishes (2026, Buenos Aires). AR dessert presentations increased dessert ordering in an Oxford restaurant experiment.
- **[INDUSTRY] Indian Gen Z is the fastest-growing dining-out cohort.** Swiggy × Kearney *How India Eats 2025* reports Gen Z dining-out growth at roughly 3× other cohorts, a 20% rise in unique cuisines per customer, a 30% rise in restaurants explored, pre-booking growing ~7× walk-ins, and 75%+ of chain marketing spend going to digital. These are platform/consultant figures, not an independent census.
- **[INDUSTRY] Social discovery is large but the numbers disagree.** A secondary trade article cites YouGov finding 72% of urban Indians discover new QSR brands on social media. A National Restaurant Association Show summary claims 78% of Gen Z and millennials consider a restaurant's Instagram/TikTok-worthiness when choosing where to eat, while another trade outlet reports 79% for millennials only. Conflicting framings of the same survey family — do not quote a single figure as fact.
- **[INDUSTRY] Dessert is a young, planned, visual occasion.** CGA by NIQ's 2026 dessert report (2,000 GB consumers) finds over nine in ten order dessert when eating out, with a third doing so on every or most visits and nearly half of 18–34s in that frequent group; frequent dessert consumers say they order on impulse only 20% of the time, and are more likely to choose on "Instagrammability". British data, but the only recent dessert-specific evidence found.
- **[WEAK] "Shareable" is not automatically better.** The small-plates format has been criticised for hiding portion size and encouraging over-ordering; the same review notes diners found it confusing and poor value (trade and press commentary, 2018–2026). There is no experimental evidence that photographable plating raises sales in Indian dine-in — **[DATA MISSING]**.

### Evidence / source summary

Photos and mental simulation are supported by experiments, mostly on stated choice or single-site field tests. Indian Gen Z data is platform and trade data, useful for direction, unusable for precise design rules.

### Practical recommendation

- **Mark 1–2 "new" or limited dishes per section** rather than creating a novelty ghetto. A small, legible signal ("new", "this month") does the work.
- **Use one strong image per section on the dish you most want to sell**, not a photo grid. Attention is finite; a pictured dish draws gaze from its neighbours (inference from attention studies, not a tested rule).
- **Write names that are sayable and visual** — a stack, a sizzle, a colour, a copper handi. If a guest cannot describe it to a friend, it will not be shared.
- **Give young guests an accessible entry price** and a small-plate or tasting option. Exploration is budget-constrained; a card where every novel dish is the most expensive suppresses trial.
- **Avoid gimmicks that raise expectations the kitchen cannot meet.** The photo effect is an expectation effect; a disappointing plate converts a trial into a negative review.

### Confidence

**Medium** that visual/experiential cues and novelty signals matter to younger guests. **Low** on any specific "Instagram" or "shareable" rule, and **Low** on the reliability of the Indian social-discovery percentages.

### Limitations

No peer-reviewed study of Indian Gen Z menu reading; platform data is self-selected and commercial; photo studies are single-site; "young" spans 18–34 with very different budgets.

## 7. Groups: order-for-the-table and sharing structures

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] Groups deliberately order more variety than individuals would.** In a Chinese restaurant in Durham, real tables chose more varied dishes than simulated tables of the same size built from the same individual choices (814 lunch slips, 2,202 diners). When ordering aloud, diners shifted away from what the person before them ordered; when choices were private, they were happier with what they ordered. Group-level variety came at a personal cost: satisfaction fell from 7.75 to 6.80, and regret rose from 12.4% to 38.4% (Ariely & Levav 2000, *Journal of Consumer Research*).
- **[EVIDENCE] The effect is not linear.** A re-analysis found choice patterns curvilinear: at high group unanimity, people conform rather than diverge (Quester & Steyer 2009). The practical implication is that a table with a strong "we always get butter chicken" norm will not explore, however good the novelty dishes are.
- **[EVIDENCE] People eat more in larger groups** (social facilitation; de Castro & Brewer 1992 and subsequent work). Larger tables order more dishes, more courses and more drinks; the menu is read as a portfolio, not a list of individual meals.
- **[INDUSTRY] The operator heuristic is ~1.5 dishes per person for sharing formats**, plus a mix of textures, temperatures and proteins, and a large-format centrepiece for four or more (chef interviews, Broadsheet 2024). This is craft advice, not evidence.
- **[CULTURAL / WEAK] Indian group ordering is share-heavy and portion-flexible.** "One by two" portions, shared curries, and thali-plus-shared-sides are normal; a Bohra *thaal* seats four to eight around one plate. These are documented practices, not menu experiments. **[DATA MISSING]** on how thali/platter presentation changes spend, satisfaction or perceived value.
- **[EVIDENCE, inference] Position effects are likely to matter more for groups than for solos.** A table scanning for a spread reads the edges of each section, so a "for the table" block probably benefits more than a single diner would. The position finding is tested; this extension to groups is reasoning from it, not a tested group result. Items at the beginning or end of a category were up to twice as popular as the same items in the middle; the average edge advantage was about 55% vs 45% (Dayan & Bar-Hillel 2011, lab plus Tel Aviv café field study). This is a layout finding and belongs with `agent4_layout.md`; here it explains why a "for the table" block at a section edge will actually be seen.

### Evidence / source summary

The group variety-seeking result is strong and has been replicated in direction, with an important boundary condition (unanimity → conformity). Sharing-format advice is industry craft. Indian thali behaviour is culturally documented but not experimentally tested.

### Practical recommendation

- **Create an explicit "for the table" or "shared plates" block** with serves counts and a per-head price guide. A group should not have to reverse-engineer how many dishes to order.
- **Design for the portfolio:** 2–3 shareable starters, 2–3 large formats, breads and rice with half/full portions, and a price ladder that lets a table of four land inside a budget without arithmetic.
- **State heaviness and spice per dish** so a table can diversify deliberately (one rich, one dry, one mild, one hot). This is the single most useful piece of information for a group and it is usually missing.
- **Give thalis a composition line** ("dal, seasonal sabzi, rice, 4 rotis, salad, papad, sweet") rather than just a price. It answers the value question and the sharing question at once.
- **Expect a "captain".** One person often orders for the table; make the card easy to scan in 60 seconds and put the recommended combination near the top of the sharing block.

### Confidence

**High** that group context increases variety and that groups read the card differently. **Medium** that sharing blocks change spend. **Low** on thali-specific effects.

### Limitations

Ariely & Levav is a US Chinese restaurant in 2000; group norms vary by culture and by who is paying; no Indian group-order dataset was found; "sharing" can be resisted by guests who want their own dish.

## 8. Impulse buyers: triggers

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] Impulse ordering is real but smaller than the folklore.** Restaurant impulse buying is defined as a sudden, immediate order without prior intention (Rook 1987; Hyun, Han & Kim 2015). Experimental work shows pre-purchase mood and affect-regulation motives (eating to feel better) jointly drive impulse buying, and that indulgence motives make sensory cues more accessible (2013, *Journal of Foodservice Business Research*).
- **[EVIDENCE] Visual presentation raises the target item's share.** Photos increased stated ordering in a 742-person experiment and raised sales shares in a café field test; AR dessert presentation increased dessert ordering in a restaurant experiment. The mechanism is mental simulation and anticipated enjoyment, not information.
- **[WEAK] Menu aesthetics may increase side-order impulse buying — and calorie labels dampen it.** A 352-respondent Taiwanese survey found aesthetically designed menus were associated with impulse buying of side orders, while calorie labelling restored a more deliberate process and reduced the behaviour (2017). Self-report and correlational.
- **[EVIDENCE] Warm colour and calorie information interact.** A red (vs blue/white) menu background induced higher arousal and temptation and led to more indulgent purchases; when calorie information was present, the effect reversed and self-control rose (2020, *Journal of Foodservice Business Research*). Lab experiment; do not over-read into print menus.
- **[EVIDENCE] Position is a reliable lever.** Items at the beginning or end of a category are chosen more often than the same items in the middle (Dayan & Bar-Hillel 2011). This is one of the few menu levers with both lab and field support.
- **[EVIDENCE] Other people trigger impulse orders.** Reference-group influence and social factors predicted impulse ordering, as did extraversion; "while dining with relatives and friends I usually order more dishes or dessert" was the highest-scoring item in that survey (Taiwanese restaurant study). Impulse is social, not only visual.
- **[INDUSTRY] Dessert is mostly planned, not impulsive.** CGA by NIQ's 2026 GB data: over nine in ten order dessert when eating out, but frequent dessert consumers order on impulse only 20% of the time. The commercial lever is menu presence and visibility before the visit, not a last-minute nudge.
- **[WEAK] Scarcity and "limited time" messages raise urge to buy** in social-commerce restaurant coupons (2015 PACIS study). Online coupon context, not dine-in menus.

### Evidence / source summary

The best-supported triggers are **visuals, position and social context**; colour and scarcity are weaker and context-bound. The "dessert is an impulse purchase" assumption is contradicted by the dessert data. Most studies measure stated choice or self-report, and effect sizes are modest.

### Practical recommendation

- **Put 2–3 high-margin impulse items at section edges** (first or last), not buried in the middle. This is the most defensible impulse lever available.
- **Use one photo per section, on the item you want to move.** Do not illustrate everything; a photo wall flattens attention and raises print cost.
- **Keep a small dessert and beverage block at the end of the card**, with one line of sensory description. Recency and visibility do more than "temptation" language.
- **Use sensory words tied to texture and temperature** ("crisp", "smoked", "cold", "sizzling"), which is also what the descriptive-label evidence supports.
- **Do not fake scarcity** ("only 2 left") on a dine-in card; it reads as pressure and damages trust, especially with premium guests.
- **Brief servers to suggest one specific item.** In the children's study, 22% named server suggestion as a trigger; in practice a server's sentence is likely stronger than any typographic trick, and it costs nothing to test.

### Confidence

**Medium** for visual and position effects. **Low** for colour, scarcity and the size of impulse spend in Indian dine-in.

### Limitations

Lab and single-site studies; self-reported impulse behaviour is unreliable; "impulse" overlaps with "undecided"; cultural norms around ordering for others change what counts as impulse.

## 9. How familiar-food seekers and new-experience seekers read a menu

### Key findings

- **[EVIDENCE] There is no universal scanpath and no reliable "sweet spot".** Eye-tracking studies find book-like reading in some menus and different patterns in others; a dedicated revisitation concluded that the industry's "sweet spots" may not exist (Penn State, 2012), and a later study found the most-fixated dish was not necessarily the one ordered (2026, fine dining, n=25). Layout changes gaze; gaze does not guarantee choice.
- **[EVIDENCE] Familiarity changes what is searched for.** Low-familiarity guests weight hedonic cues (experience, atmosphere, novelty); high-familiarity guests weight utilitarian cues (food quality, value) (Ha & Jang 2010). In practice: the new-experience seeker reads descriptions and provenance; the familiar seeker reads names, portions and prices.
- **[EVIDENCE] Price-conscious guests look differently, not just longer** (Ngan et al. 2022). A menu designed for one reading style will disadvantage the other.
- **[EVIDENCE] Names dominate attention.** In a small eye-tracking study, dish names drew the longest fixation, ahead of price and image; images drew attention mainly when present. This supports a name-first hierarchy.
- **[WEAK] "Familiarity" can be manufactured.** Adding familiar flavours, appearance and preparation to a novel dish lowered the barrier for US consumers (Hwang & Lin 2010). The effect is real but modest; it does not turn a neophobe into an explorer.

### Practical recommendation

- **Use a fixed three-part rhythm per dish: name → one clause → price.** The familiar seeker can stop at the name; the explorer reads the clause; both find the price in the same place.
- **Keep classic names intact and put the novelty in the clause.** "Dal makhani, slow-cooked overnight on charcoal" works for both. "Charcoal-kissed black lentil velouté" works for neither.
- **Signal adventure at section level** with a short subtitle ("Tandoor & grills", "Regional specials", "For the table") rather than labelling dishes "exotic".
- **Do not force every dish to carry a story.** A uniform wall of description raises reading cost and hides the two dishes you actually want to sell.

### Confidence

**Medium.** The trait and familiarity evidence is solid; the reading-rhythm recommendation is an inference from it, not a tested intervention.

### Limitations

Eye-tracking samples are small (often 13–35 participants) and lab-based; no Indian scanpath study; reading behaviour differs on a phone versus a printed A4 card.

## 11. What the evidence does not support

These are commonly repeated as rules. They are not supported strongly enough to design around, and several are contradicted:

1. **"The golden triangle / top-right corner is where the eye goes."** Eye-tracking finds no consistent sweet spot; scanpaths vary by menu, layout and price consciousness (2012 eye-tracking revisitation; Ngan et al. 2022). Use layout for reading ease, not for a mythical hotspot.
2. **"Dollar signs cost you 8%."** One US lunch study found 8.15% higher per-person spend with numeral-only prices, but party size and dwell time explained far more variance, and it has not been replicated in India (Yang, Kimes & Sessarego 2009). Do not quote 8% as a law.
3. **"Descriptive labels lift sales 27%."** One six-week cafeteria study with 140 customers and six items found 27% higher unit sales, plus better quality/value ratings — but no increase in willingness to pay (Wansink, Painter & van Ittersum 2001). It is a real finding in one setting, not a universal multiplier.
4. **"Long menus paralyse customers."** Meta-analysis finds the average choice-overload effect is essentially zero (Scheibehenne et al. 2010). Shorten for operations, not for psychology.
5. **"Dessert is an impulse purchase."** In recent GB data, frequent dessert consumers order on impulse only 20% of the time (CGA/NIQ 2026). Dessert is largely planned; visibility before and during the visit matters more than a last-second nudge.
6. **"Pictures always sell."** Photos raise the pictured item's share, but they also draw attention from neighbours, and one café field test found no effect for one of three dishes. Use one, deliberately.
7. **"Young people only want novelty."** The same 2026 NRA forecast that ranks global flavours high also ranks comfort food second and value third. Novelty and nostalgia are running together, not replacing each other.

## 12. Evidence gaps and how to close them in-house

- **No Indian dine-in menu experiment** on price presentation, description length, photo density or thali framing was found. This is the biggest gap in the whole brief. **[DATA MISSING]**
- **No Indian data on price thresholds** (₹99 vs ₹100, ₹249 vs ₹250, acceptable spend by city tier). Any rule here is imported. **[DATA MISSING]**
- **No data on multi-generational Indian tables** — who holds the menu, who decides, whether children or elders dominate. **[DATA MISSING]**
- **No measured effect of sharing/platter formats on Indian spend or satisfaction.** **[DATA MISSING]**

Cheap ways to close them, in order of value:

1. **Run a 4-week A/B on the printed card** (two versions, alternate by week or by table section), and measure *item mix and average cover*, not just revenue. Two to four weeks per cell is the minimum before seasonality ruins the comparison.
2. **Record what guests ask servers.** "What is this?", "Is it spicy?", "How many pieces?", "Is this enough for two?" are free qualitative data and map directly to the sections above.
3. **Track send-backs and uneaten plates** on new dishes. A novelty dish with high send-back is an expectation failure, not a demand failure.
4. **Ask at the till, not online.** A three-question card ("What did you almost order? What was hard to find? Was the price clear?") gets better data from Indian dine-in guests than a QR survey.

Do not A/B the whole card at once. Change one lever per test, or you will learn nothing.
