# Agent 1 — Consumer Psychology & Decision-Making on Menus

**Scope:** how diners choose from menus, decision load, attention, priming/anchoring, decoys, popularity/scarcity cues, descriptions, and perceived expensiveness. **Not** a menu design.

**Evidence labels used throughout**

- **[FIELD]** — real customers, real money, real orders.
- **[LAB]** — controlled experiment, usually hypothetical choices or intentions.
- **[META]** — meta-analysis / systematic review.
- **[INDUSTRY]** — trade claim or consultant assertion, not a study.
- **[WEAK]** — evidence thin, indirect, or contradicted.
- **[MISSING]** — no usable evidence found.

**Headline caveat for the whole report:** the best menu-design meta-analysis finds that menu design has a **large effect on physiological/attention measures, a moderate effect on stated intention and attitude, and only a small ("marginal") effect on actual purchase** (Ip & Chark, 2023; 53 papers, n = 16,522). Every number below should be read through that filter. Effects measured on intentions are systematically larger than effects measured on orders.

## 1. How customers choose food from menus: sequence, satisficing vs optimising

### Key findings

1. **Diners do not read a menu. They run a short search, then stop.** In a 2024 mixed-methods study, diners reported 16 distinct meal-choice strategies, and the most-used were: search the menu for **(a) the most enjoyable dish, (b) the most budget-friendly dish, (c) a familiar dish** — then choose from that shortlist. Many people used more than one strategy in the same meal. [FIELD-adjacent: interviews + survey]
2. **The shortlist is the decision.** Choice is best modelled as two stages: (i) build a small consideration set by filtering on one dominant goal (taste/indulgence, price, familiarity, dietary constraint), then (ii) compare only within that set. This is **satisficing** — "good enough on my main criterion" — not maximising across all attributes (Simon, 1955; Payne, Bettman & Johnson, 1993).
3. **The first filter is usually not price.** In a 1,208-person Australian best–worst study of casual and fine dining, **"combination of ingredients" was the most influential attribute** in menu-item selection; avoidance of certain foods (allergens, dislikes, dietary rules) was also highly influential. Price matters, but it is rarely the first gate.
4. **Eye movements follow reading, not a treasure map.** Diners read a two-page menu roughly like a book — left page first, top to bottom — and stop when the relevant section ends (Yang, 2012). See §3.
5. **Menu length changes the strategy, not just the mood.** With very long lists, diners fall back on the familiar/default dish rather than comparing (consistent with choice-overload moderators in §2). [WEAK for menu specifically — inferred, not directly measured in a menu field trial]

### Evidence / sources

- Greene, D., Nguyen, M., & Dolnicar, S. (2024). *How do you choose your meal when you dine out? A mixed methods study in consumer food-choice strategies in the restaurant context.* **Appetite**, 203, 107683. 21 interviews → 16 strategies; survey quantified frequency. [peer-reviewed]
- Mueller Loose & Szolnoki-type attribute work is not cited here; the attribute result above is from the Best–Worst study of 1,208 Australian patrons reported via AGRIS/FAO record (2023–24). [peer-reviewed, abstract-level verification only]
- Simon, H. A. (1955). A behavioral model of rational choice. **Quarterly Journal of Economics**. Classic theory of satisficing.
- Payne, J. W., Bettman, J. R., & Johnson, E. J. (1993). **The Adaptive Decision Maker**. Strategy selection under effort/accuracy trade-offs.
- Yang, S. S. (2012). Eye movements on restaurant menus. **International Journal of Hospitality Management**, 31(3), 1021–1029.

**What is NOT supported:** the common claim that "diners decide in 109 seconds" or "read the menu for 109 seconds." No source located for that figure. [MISSING]

### Practical recommendation

Assume the guest is running a one-criterion filter, not a full comparison. Make each dish's **lead benefit obvious in the first 3–6 words** (what it is, what it tastes like, whether it is vegetarian/spicy) so the filter can be applied without reading the whole entry. Do not rely on the guest reading every description.

### Confidence

**Medium.** The strategy finding is recent and well-designed but interview/survey-based (stated, not observed). The two-stage/satisficing model is strong general theory with only indirect menu evidence.

### Limitations

- Stated strategies may not match in-the-moment behaviour.
- The Australian sample may not transfer to Indian diners; no India-specific decision-sequence study was found. [MISSING]

## 2. Decision fatigue and choice overload

### Key findings

1. **The famous jam study is real, but it is a demonstration, not a law.** Iyengar & Lepper (2000) ran a tasting booth at Draeger's supermarket: 24 jams attracted more stoppers (**~60% vs ~40%**) but produced far fewer purchases (**~3% vs ~30%** of stoppers). Effects replicated in chocolates and essay-topic choice. [FIELD + LAB]
2. **The meta-analytic average is ~zero.** Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010) pooled **63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036)**: mean effect **D = 0.02, 95% CI −0.09 to 0.12**. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² ≈ 68%), but **no sufficient condition** for overload could be identified. [META]
3. **Overload is conditional, not universal.** Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015) pooled **99 observations (N = 7,202)** and found four reliable moderators: **choice-set complexity, decision-task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and a prominent effort-minimising goal**. When these are accounted for, the overall effect of assortment size is significant. In plain terms: overload appears when options are hard to compare, the chooser does not know their own preference, time/effort is scarce, and the goal is "just pick something." [META]
4. **The brain shows an inverted-U, with ~12 options as the sweet spot in that task.** Reutskaja et al. (2018, *Nature Human Behaviour*) had participants choose from sets of 6, 12, or 24 images while in an fMRI. Activity in dorsal striatum and anterior cingulate cortex peaked at **12**; 6 and 24 were both rated as wrong-sized ("too few"/"too many"). This is a lab task with pictures, **not** a restaurant menu. [LAB]
5. **Menu-specific evidence exists but is thin and survey-based.** Johns, Edwards & Hartwell (2013) found diners report an **ideal of about 6 choices for quick-service and roughly 7–10 for fine dining**. That is a stated preference, not an order-rate experiment. [WEAK–MEDIUM]
6. **Culture matters — and this is directly relevant to India.** Reutskaja, Cheek, Iyengar & Schwartz (2022; six nations incl. India) found **choice deprivation (too few options) was more common and more consequential than overload**, and that overload was far rarer outside Western countries. Indian participants reported overload in roughly **5%** of choice situations. [META-adjacent cross-national survey]
7. **"Decision fatigue" as depletion of willpower is not a solid basis for menu claims.** The largest preregistered multi-lab test of ego depletion (Hagger et al., 2016; 23 labs, N = 2,141) found **d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.15** — indistinguishable from zero. The popular "judges grant parole after lunch" story has also been heavily criticised on case-ordering grounds. [META]
8. **What survives is narrower and more useful:** long, hard-to-compare lists increase **deferral, default-seeking, and regret**, and decrease confidence — especially under time pressure. That is "choice overload," not "willpower running out."

### Evidence / sources

- Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating. **JPSP**, 79(6), 995–1006.
- Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? **Journal of Consumer Research**, 37(3), 409–425.
- Chernev, A., Böckenholt, U., & Goodman, J. (2015). Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis. **Journal of Consumer Psychology**, 25(2), 333–358.
- Reutskaja, E., Lindner, A., Nagel, R., Andersen, R. A., & Camerer, C. F. (2018). **Nature Human Behaviour**, 2(12), 925–935.
- Johns, N., Edwards, J. S. A., & Hartwell, H. (2013). Menu choice: Satisfaction or overload? **Journal of Culinary Science & Technology**, 11(3), 275–285.
- Reutskaja, E., Cheek, N. N., Iyengar, S., & Schwartz, B. (2022). Choice deprivation, choice overload, and satisfaction with choices across six nations. **Journal of International Marketing**, 30(3), 18–34.
- Hagger, M. S., et al. (2016). A multilab preregistered replication of the ego-depletion effect. **Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 11(4), 546–573.

**Data gaps:** No peer-reviewed field experiment on **number of dishes on a restaurant menu and order rate / spend** was found. The "6–7 items per category" and "12 options" rules circulating in trade media are extrapolations, not restaurant findings. [MISSING for the field experiment]

### Practical recommendation

Do not treat "shorter is always better" as proven. Treat **comparability** as the real lever: fewer near-duplicate dishes, clearer category logic, and a visible organising principle (e.g., by main ingredient or cooking method) reduce the difficulty that actually drives overload. For an Indian menu with many similar curry variants, the risk is not item count per se but **unordered similarity**.

### Confidence

**High** that overload is conditional and that the average effect is small. **Medium** that a menu of roughly 6–10 items per section is a reasonable operating range. **Low** for any claim that a specific number of items will raise revenue.

### Limitations

- Almost all overload studies use unfamiliar, low-stakes items (jams, chocolates, pictures); restaurant dishes are familiar and high-involvement.
- Menu studies measure stated ideals, not orders.
- Cross-cultural findings mean Western "less choice" advice may misfire in India, where variety can signal hospitality and value.

## 3. Attention and visual scanning on menus

### Key findings

1. **The "golden triangle" (centre → top-right → top-left) is not supported by the peer-reviewed eye-tracking evidence.** Yang (2012) found diners read a two-page menu **sequentially, like a book** (left page before right), and found **no area that received more fixations than any other** — i.e. no "sweet spot." There was weak evidence of a **"sour spot"** in one region with fewer fixations (restaurant info/salad list). [LAB, eye-tracking]
2. **"Sweet spots" may exist, but they depend on layout, not anatomy.** Kuo, Bavik, Ngan & Yu (2020/2021) tested four Asian restaurant menus and found sweet and sour spots on **three of four** menus; page orientation and text direction changed the pattern. [LAB]
3. **Where people look depends on what they care about.** Ngan, Kuo & Yu (2022) found visual patterns differ by **price-consciousness** — price-focused diners look at prices more. Attention is not a fixed heat map. [LAB]
4. **Position within a list is a real, replicated effect — much better evidenced than "hot spots."** Dayan & Bar-Hillel (2011) found items placed at the **beginning or end of their category list were up to twice as popular** as the same item placed in the middle. This held in a lab study and in a real restaurant. [LAB + FIELD]
5. **Gaze does not equal purchase.** At least one eye-tracking study reports that the first fixation area did not match the area generating the most purchases, and that gaze order did not directly predict buying — a useful caution against over-reading heat maps. [WEAK: single study, reported second-hand]
6. **Visuals are not automatically attention magnets.** A menu eye-tracking study found that adding images was "not fully effective" in directing attention. [WEAK]

### Evidence / sources

- Yang, S. S. (2012). **IJHM**, 31(3), 1021–1029.
- Kuo, C.-F., Bavik, A., Ngan, H. F. B., & Yu, C.-E. (2020/2021). The sweet spot in the eye of the beholder? **Journal of Hospitality Marketing & Management**.
- Ngan, H. F. B., Kuo, C.-F., & Yu, C.-E. (2022). Where you look depends on what you are willing to afford. **Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research**, 46(1), 100–124.
- Dayan, E., & Bar-Hillel, M. (2011). Nudge to nobesity II: Menu positions influence food orders. **Judgment and Decision Making**, 6(4), 333–342.

**Industry claim explicitly contradicted:** "Customers' eyes go centre → top right → top left; put high-margin items there." Widely repeated by menu-consulting firms; the peer-reviewed eye-tracking studies do not find one universal path. [INDUSTRY, contradicted]

### Practical recommendation

Rely on **serial position**, not on mythical hot corners: the **first and last item in each section** get disproportionate attention and orders. Put the dishes you want considered at those two positions. Design for a **book-like scan**: clear section headers, consistent item order, and enough whitespace that the eye can find the start of each section.

### Confidence

**High** that the golden triangle is not established science. **Medium–High** for first/last position effects. **Medium** that layout-specific sweet spots exist but vary.

### Limitations

- Eye-tracking samples are small (often 30–60 participants) and use one or two menus.
- Lab menus lack hunger, social pressure, and price anchoring from a real bill.
- Position effects can decay with repeated visits (regulars learn the menu).

## 4. Priming and anchoring

### Key findings

1. **Anchoring is one of the most robust effects in psychology — in general pricing.** Exposure to an arbitrary number shifts willingness to pay (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec, 2003, "coherent arbitrariness": valuations anchor on a random number and stay coherent around it). [LAB, very strong]
2. **Direct menu-specific anchoring evidence is surprisingly thin.** The widely repeated claim "put a very expensive dish first so everything else looks cheap" is asserted by essentially every menu-consulting source, but I could not locate a rigorous peer-reviewed **field experiment** on a real restaurant menu testing a high-price anchor against a control. One 2025 conference paper (Atlantis Press, ICHCH) claims a natural experiment with 3,000 Beijing orders and a difference-in-differences design; it is not a peer-reviewed journal article and its methods cannot be verified. Treat the menu claim as **plausible but unproven**. [INDUSTRY / WEAK]
3. **The nearest strong menu evidence is price-format, not anchor-item.** See §8: removing currency symbols changed spend in a real restaurant (Yang, Kimes & Sessarego, 2009). That is a framing/pain-of-paying effect, not an anchor effect. [FIELD]
4. **Priming, as usually described, mostly fails in food field tests.** A 2022 study with both a field study (food logos → snack choice) and a lab study found **no main effect of priming** on food choice. The broader "social priming" literature has well-documented replication failures. [FIELD + LAB, null]
5. **What people call "priming" on menus is usually order and salience.** The reliable version is: what is seen first, what is set apart, and what is labelled — not subliminal activation. [Synthesis]
6. **A real, if mundane, priming-adjacent effect:** reading a description that uses sensory/indulgent language changes what people expect and choose (see §7). That is semantic framing, not unconscious priming. [FIELD]

### Evidence / sources

- Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. **Science**, 185, 1124–1131.
- Ariely, D., Loewenstein, G., & Prelec, D. (2003). "Coherent arbitrariness": Stable demand curves without stable preferences. **Quarterly Journal of Economics**, 118(1), 73–105.
- Yang, S. S., Kimes, S. E., & Sessarego, M. M. (2009). Menu price presentation influences on consumer purchase behavior in restaurants. **IJHM**, 28(1), 157–160.
- *The effect of priming on food choice: A field and laboratory study.* **Appetite** (2022), 10.1016/j.appet.2021.105749.
- Unverified: Atlantis Press proceedings, "Mechanism of Price Anchoring in Dining Consumption Decision-Making" (2025). [WEAK, not peer-reviewed]

**Explicit data gap:** no peer-reviewed field test of "high-priced anchor item raises mid-priced item sales" on a restaurant menu. [MISSING]

### Practical recommendation

Do not budget on anchoring as if it were proven. If a high-priced dish is included, include it because it is a legitimate offer (a sharing platter, a tasting menu, a premium biryani), not as a purely decorative anchor. Do not build the price architecture around a decorative item you would be embarrassed to serve.

### Confidence

**High** for anchoring in general. **Low** for the specific menu-anchor tactic. **High** that deliberate priming effects on food choice are unreliable.

### Limitations

- General anchoring studies use arbitrary numbers and hypothetical WTP; restaurant menus give real, informative prices, which weakens anchoring (people can compare).
- The menu-anchor claim is propagated almost entirely by self-interested industry sources.

## 5. Decoy effect (asymmetric dominance)

### Key findings

1. **The original effect is real in the lab.** Huber, Payne & Puto (1982) showed that adding an option **asymmetrically dominated** by one target (worse than the target on all attributes, better than the competitor on one) increased the target's share — a violation of the independence of irrelevant alternatives. [LAB]
2. **It is much weaker, and often absent, outside tidy lab choices.** Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014, *Journal of Marketing Research*) ran a large series and **found no attraction effect in any of their studies** with more realistic stimuli; some showed a *repulsion* effect. Yang & Lynn (2014) made **91 attempts across 23 product classes and got only 11 reliable effects** — far fewer than expected. The original authors responded defending boundary conditions (Huber, Payne & Puto, 2014). [LAB; contested]
3. **Food-specific decoy evidence is recent, online, and intention-based.** A 2025 randomized online experiment added a **more expensive non-meat "decoy"** to a visual menu and found increased selection of the target non-meat menu and reduced meat selection, with eye-tracking. It is an online purchase-intention study, not a restaurant sales test. [LAB]
4. **For a normal restaurant menu, the decoy is hard to construct honestly.** A decoy must be clearly worse on the attributes the guest is weighing. On a menu, a "worse" dish is usually just a dish nobody wants, and guests can see that. The effect also requires the chooser to be **near-indifferent** between two options — rare when someone is choosing dinner. [Synthesis]

### Evidence / sources

- Huber, J., Payne, J. W., & Puto, C. (1982). Adding asymmetrically dominated alternatives. **Journal of Consumer Research**, 9(1), 90–98.
- Frederick, S., Lee, L., & Baskin, E. (2014). The limits of attraction. **JMR**, 51(4), 487–507.
- Yang, S., & Lynn, M. (2014). More evidence challenging the robustness and usefulness of the attraction effect. **JMR**, 51(4), 508–513.
- Huber, Payne & Puto (2014). Let's be honest about the attraction effect. **JMR**, 51(4), 520–525.
- 2025 online decoy/menu experiment, **International Journal of Gastronomy and Food Science** (ScienceDirect PII S1878450X25002446). [LAB]

**Data gap:** no field experiment showing a decoy dish raises sales of another dish in a real restaurant. [MISSING]

### Practical recommendation

Do not add a deliberately bad dish. If the menu naturally contains a **premium, clearly larger/better version** of a popular dish (e.g., a full vs. half portion, or a thali vs. a single curry), the comparison is honest and may still help. That is the only defensible use of the principle.

### Confidence

**High** that the original lab effect exists. **Low** that it transfers reliably to real menu choice. **Low–Medium** for any specific decoy tactic.

### Limitations

- Replication failures are substantial and the debate is unresolved.
- Menu choices are multi-attribute and familiar; decoy studies use two attributes and unfamiliar items.

## 6. Scarcity and popularity cues

### Key findings

1. **"Most popular" works — when it is true and when the guest lacks prior knowledge.** Cai, Chen & Fang (2009, *American Economic Review*) ran a randomized field experiment in a Beijing restaurant chain. Showing diners the **ranking of the five most popular dishes increased demand for those dishes by 13–20%**. Merely naming five "sample dishes" (same salience, no ranking) had **no significant effect**. The effect was stronger among **infrequent customers**. This cleanly separates social proof from mere visual salience. [FIELD — strong]
2. **"Chef's choice" did not work in a later field test.** Bookwala, Gallemore & Gómez-Miñambres (2022, *Economic Inquiry*) ran a randomized field experiment at two food festivals. **"Most popular" was the only recommendation that significantly increased demand**; "chef's choice" did not. The effect appeared **only among customers from outside the local region** — again, social proof substitutes for prior knowledge. [FIELD]
3. **Scarcity delivered by a person beats scarcity printed on the menu.** Nazlan, Tanford, Raab & Choi (2018) manipulated scarcity cues, bundling, and price in a restaurant scenario. **Server-delivered scarcity influenced choice more than menu-printed scarcity.** Bundled pricing *reduced* selection of the limited-quantity item, and guests would pay more for a scarce item when the scarcity message came from the server. [LAB/scenario]
4. **Scarcity is a claim about truth.** "Only 3 left today" on a dish that is always available is a false statement. In India, this also runs into consumer-protection norms on misleading commercial claims. The evidence supports **true** popularity and **true** limited availability; it does not support fabricated urgency. [Synthesis + legal caution]
5. **Popularity cues can backfire on positioning.** If every section is labelled "most popular," the label carries no information and the effect should collapse. No study directly tests saturation; this is inference. [WEAK]

### Evidence / sources

- Cai, H., Chen, Y., & Fang, H. (2009). Observational learning: Evidence from a randomized natural field experiment. **American Economic Review**, 99(3), 864–882.
- Bookwala, K., Gallemore, C., & Gómez-Miñambres, J. (2022). The influence of food recommendations: Evidence from a randomized field experiment. **Economic Inquiry**.
- Nazlan, N. H., Tanford, S., Raab, C., & Choi, C. C. (2018). The influence of scarcity cues and price bundling on menu item selection. **Journal of Foodservice Business Research**, 21(4), 420–439.

### Practical recommendation

Use **one** honest social-proof signal per section at most (e.g., "most ordered" on a single dish where you actually have the sales data), and make sure it is true and current. Prefer staff-delivered scarcity ("we have two portions of the kebab left") over printed urgency. Do not label everything "chef's special."

### Confidence

**High** for true "most popular" cues in unfamiliar customers. **Medium** that effects fade for regulars. **Low** for printed scarcity labels.

### Limitations

- Cai et al. is a single chain in Beijing, 2009; Bookwala et al. is food festivals, not a sit-down restaurant.
- Both effects are strongest for **new** customers — exactly the ones a menu redesign is usually aimed at, but the effect may not persist.
- Nazlan et al. is a scenario experiment, not real orders.

## 7. Menu descriptions and labelling (including the Wansink problem)

### Key findings

1. **The Wansink menu-label literature cannot be relied on.** Brian Wansink's Cornell Food & Brand Lab produced the most-cited "descriptive menu labels increase sales" numbers (e.g., Wansink, Painter & van Ittersum, 2001: descriptive labels → ~27% more orders and ~10% higher willingness to pay, from 150 diners). Cornell's 2018 investigation found **academic misconduct** including misreported data and problematic statistics; **13 of his papers were retracted** (seven before September 2018, six JAMA-network papers on 19 September 2018). The 2001 menu-label paper is not among the confirmed retractions I could verify, but it comes from the same lab, era, and data practices, and its specific numbers should **not** be quoted as fact. [Retracted/corrupted evidence base]
2. **Independent evidence supports the *direction* of the description effect.** Turnwald, Boles & Crum (2017, *JAMA Internal Medicine*) ran a 46-day cafeteria study (27,933 diners; 8,279 chose the featured vegetable). The same vegetables were labelled **basic**, **healthy-restrictive**, **healthy-positive**, or **indulgent** ("Twisted citrus-glazed carrots", "Dynamite chili and tangy lime-seasoned beets"). **Indulgent labels produced 25% more takers than basic labels, 41% more than healthy-restrictive, and 35% more than healthy-positive**; mass of vegetables taken rose 23% vs basic and 33% vs healthy-restrictive. Preparation did not change. [FIELD, quasi-random daily assignment]
3. **This replicates in other settings, with caveats.** Gavrieli et al. (2022, *BMC Public Health*) ran a quasi-experiment in four workplace cafeterias (Chicago, Sydney, São Paulo, Singapore). **Appealing dish names increased food taken per plate by 43.9% relative to baseline** versus basic names (+54.5% vs +10.6%), equivalent to about **7% more grams per plate**. The effect was **site-specific to English-speaking countries only** — an important limit for India. [FIELD-ish, quasi-experimental]
4. **"Healthy" language actively hurts.** Turnwald, Jurafsky, Conner & Crum (2017, *Health Psychology*) analysed 26 US casual-dining chains: healthy-section items (5,873 words) were described with **significantly less appealing** language than standard items (38,343 words) — less "tasty", "indulgent", "spicy", "artisanal"; more "fresh", "simple", "nutritious", "deprivation". [Content analysis, large corpus]
5. **Descriptions affect expectations and willingness to pay, but the WTP numbers are softer than the selection numbers.** Studies consistently find descriptive/sensory labels raise expected taste and stated WTP; the strongest real-behaviour evidence is on **selection**, not on price paid. [Mixed]
6. **Texture and sensory descriptors are a live but early area.** A 2023 *British Food Journal* study found sensory descriptors affect intention to visit and word-of-mouth; it measured intentions, not orders. [LAB]

### Evidence / sources

- Wansink, B., Painter, J. M., & van Ittersum, K. (2001). Descriptive menu labels' effect on sales. **Cornell Hotel and Restaurant Administration Quarterly**, 42(6), 68–72. **Do not cite the numbers.**
- Cornell misconduct finding and retractions: Retraction Watch (2017–2018); *JAMA* network retractions (19 Sept 2018); *BMJ* "Open science prevents mindless science" (2018); AP/Time coverage. 13 retractions total as of Oct 2018.
- Turnwald, B. P., Boles, D. Z., & Crum, A. J. (2017). **JAMA Internal Medicine**, 177(8), 1216–1218. (Correction issued 24 July 2017 for two swapped P values; conclusions unchanged.)
- Turnwald, B. P., Jurafsky, D., Conner, A., & Crum, A. J. (2017). Reading between the menu lines. **Health Psychology**, 36(11), 1034–1037.
- Gavrieli, A., et al. (2022). Appealing dish names to nudge diners to more sustainable food choices. **BMC Public Health**, 22, 2109.
- *Is texture the new taste?* **British Food Journal** (2023), 125(10).

### Practical recommendation

Describe dishes in **concrete, sensory, enjoyable language** — what it tastes like, how it is cooked, what it is served with — rather than in health or virtue language. Do not use invented premium adjectives with no informational content. Keep descriptions honest: the effect comes from making the dish **imaginable and appealing**, not from misleading the guest about ingredients.

### Confidence

**High** that appealing/sensory descriptions increase selection. **Medium** that they increase price paid. **High** that the Wansink-specific menu numbers should be discarded. **Low** on transfer of the Gavrieli effect to India (site-specific null outside English-speaking sites).

### Limitations

- Turnwald is a university cafeteria, not a commercial restaurant; the "indulgent" advantage may shrink when guests are paying.
- Gavrieli's effect was null in São Paulo and Singapore — cultural/linguistic moderation is real.
- Description effects are confounded with attention: a longer description is also a more visually salient menu entry.

## 8. Perceived expensiveness vs affordability

### Key findings

1. **Price format changes spend — with a real but modest effect.** Yang, Kimes & Sessarego (2009) randomly assigned 201 lunch diners at the Culinary Institute of America's St Andrew's to menus with `$20.00`, `20`, or `twenty dollars`. The **numeral-only** menu produced **$5.55 more per check (about 8%)** than the other formats. This is a single restaurant, one meal period, n = 201. [FIELD]
2. **Nine-ending prices have a small, real effect on choice — smaller than folklore claims.** Guéguen, Jacob, Legoherel & Ngobo (2009) ran a six-week field study in a French pizzeria-grill with **1,271 patrons**: items priced with 9-endings were about **16% more likely** to be ordered than 0-ending equivalents. A 2024 meta-analysis (Troll, Frankenbach, Friese & Loschelder, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 34(2), 299–325) found just-below prices have an **overall positive but small** effect on purchase decisions and price perception, and **no significant effect on perceived quality**. [FIELD + META]
3. **Price signals quality, but only modestly.** Rao & Monroe's (1989) meta-analysis found a **positive, statistically significant, but modest** price–perceived-quality relationship, strongest when other quality cues are absent and for expensive, infrequently purchased products. Applied to a menu: price alone will not convince anyone a dish is good, but a suspiciously cheap dish in a premium context can raise doubt. [META]
4. **Reference prices are the real mechanism.** Guests do not judge a price in isolation; they compare it with the rest of the menu and with their expectation for that restaurant type. This is well established in pricing psychology, but there is **no peer-reviewed field study** isolating "the effect of the menu's own price range on perceived expensiveness." [Theory strong, menu application WEAK]
5. **The "pain of paying" account fits the currency-symbol result.** Making the price look less like money reduces the aversive sense of paying (Prelec & Loewenstein, 1998). This is a plausible mechanism, but the 2009 study is a single test, and later replications in other contexts are mixed. [WEAK–MEDIUM]
6. **Two popular claims have no evidence I could find.** (a) "Guests avoid the cheapest item, so never put your lowest price first." (b) "The second-cheapest wine is the most ordered." Both are repeated constantly in trade media; neither has a peer-reviewed source. Treat as **[INDUSTRY], [MISSING]**. [The second-cheapest-wine claim does have a plausible mechanism — extremeness aversion — but no restaurant sales data was located.]

### Evidence / sources

- Yang, S. S., Kimes, S. E., & Sessarego, M. M. (2009). **IJHM**, 28(1), 157–160.
- Guéguen, N., Jacob, C., Legoherel, P., & Ngobo, P. (2009). Nine-ending prices and consumer's behavior: A field study in a restaurant. **IJHM**, 28(1).
- Troll, E. S., Frankenbach, J., Friese, M., & Loschelder, D. D. (2024). A meta-analysis on the effects of just-below versus round prices. **Journal of Consumer Psychology**, 34(2), 299–325.
- Rao, A. R., & Monroe, K. B. (1989). The effect of price, brand name, and store name on buyers' perceptions of product quality. **Journal of Marketing Research**, 26(3), 351–360.
- Prelec, D., & Loewenstein, G. (1998). The red and the black: Mental accounting of savings and debt. **Marketing Science**, 17(1), 4–28.

### Practical recommendation

Decide the price **architecture** (what the restaurant is signalling) before deciding price **typography**. A consistent format across the menu matters more than any single trick. Do not rely on 9-endings to carry a price increase; the meta-analytic effect is small and does not improve perceived quality. If a dish is genuinely premium, its price can be round and confident; if the menu is value-positioned, just-below pricing is defensible.

### Confidence

**High** that price is judged relatively, within the menu. **Medium** for the currency-symbol effect (single field study). **Medium** that 9-endings help choice slightly. **Low** for any claim about "cheapest item avoidance" or "second-cheapest wine."

### Limitations

- The currency-symbol study is 200 diners in one US restaurant; a ₹ symbol in India may behave differently, and the result has not been replicated at scale.
- Guéguen et al. is one small French pizzeria; effects may reflect the unusual practice of mixing endings within one menu.
- Price-ending effects interact with brand positioning; the meta-analysis finds no quality-perception benefit.

## 10. Cross-cutting limitations

1. **Almost everything is Western.** The strongest field studies are US, French, Israeli, and Chinese. India-specific menu psychology research is scarce; the main India-relevant finding is that **choice deprivation, not overload, is the more common complaint**, and that overload is rarer there (Reutskaja et al., 2022). An Indian menu should not be shrunk on the basis of US jam studies.
2. **Intentions inflate effects.** Ip & Chark (2023) show the effect of menu design is moderate on attitudes and only marginal on actual purchase. Any study measuring "willingness to pay" or "purchase intention" overstates what will happen on the floor.
3. **Single-restaurant studies dominate.** Yang et al. (2009), Guéguen et al. (2009), and Cai et al. (2009) are each one venue or one chain. Treat effect sizes as existence proofs, not forecasts.
4. **Publication and consultant bias.** The "menu psychology" trade literature systematically repeats a small set of claims (golden triangle, 27% description lift, decoy magic, anchor item) that trace back to weak, retracted, or absent sources. This report deliberately downgrades them.
5. **Ethics.** Fabricated scarcity, fake "most popular" labels, and decoy dishes that exist only to manipulate are deceptive. Beyond ethics, they are legally risky in India under consumer-protection rules on misleading claims.
