# Agent 6 — Indian Restaurant & Cultural Context
## Research input for redesigning an Indian restaurant A4 menu card

**Prepared:** 2026
**Scope:** Indian customer expectations, menu conventions, FSSAI rules, spice/veg signalling, regional presentation, family dining, price sensitivity, common menu-design failures.
**Method note:** Every section separates *evidence-backed* findings (regulation text, peer-reviewed studies, large surveys, platform data) from *industry reporting / practitioner observation* (trade press, POS-vendor blogs, restaurant-supply companies). No statistics have been invented. Where no usable source exists, it says **DATA MISSING / WEAK EVIDENCE**.

---

## 1. What Indian customers expect from a menu

### Key findings
- **The veg/non-veg question is the first filter, not a detail.** India is the only major market where a statutory symbol system exists specifically so a diner can screen every dish at a glance. Any menu that makes this slow fails at the first task.
- **Vegetarianism is large, regionally uneven, and stricter than the headline number suggests.** Self-identification runs ~39% nationally but ranges from ~18% (east) to ~57% (west). Consumption-based measures run lower, but *behavioural strictness* among those who are vegetarian runs higher than in Western markets: a substantial share of vegetarian Indians refuse to eat in restaurants that serve meat at all.
- **Value is read as "how much did I get for the rupee", not "what was cheapest".** Indian diners in 2025–26 are described by operators as trading up to *perceived abundance* (more components, shareable, a full meal) at everyday price points rather than hunting the lowest number.
- **Variety is expected but is not the same as length.** Trade guidance converges on tight sections; Livemint's 2026 reporting on Indian menu design describes a shift toward shorter, more confident menus after years of "something for everyone" encyclopaedias.

### Evidence / source summary
| Finding | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 39% of Indian adults self-identify as vegetarian; 81% follow some meat restriction; 18%–57% by region | Pew Research Center, *Religion in India* (n=29,999, fielded Nov 2019–Mar 2020, published 2021) | Large national survey (self-report) |
| Consumption-based estimates are lower; Natrajan & Jacob (EPW, 2018) put actual vegetarianism near 20% | EPW 2018, summarised in The Wire (2018); NFHS-5 figures cited in secondary coverage | Academic + secondary |
| Only ~37% of Indian vegetarians say they would ever eat in a restaurant serving both veg and non-veg; ~40% would eat in a non-veg friend's home | Pew data as reported by Hindustan Times (20 Mar 2024) | Survey, secondary reporting |
| Diners now ask "which option gives most for my money", with ₹99/₹199 as entry points; "value through abundance" | Sagar Daryani (President, NRAI), ET HospitalityWorld, 2025–26 | Industry leader opinion piece |
| 20–30% of online delivery orders fall under ₹200; platforms re-focused on sub-₹200 | Financial Express, 13 Jul 2025 | Industry reporting |
| Indian menus becoming concise, less essay-like | Livemint, 13 Feb 2026 | Industry reporting |

### Practical recommendation
Assume a mixed table at every sitting: at least one strict vegetarian, often one Jain diner, and meat-eaters — all ordering from the same card. Build the menu so the vegetarian answer is available in under five seconds without reading descriptions. Treat "value" as *visible completeness* (what comes with the dish, whether it is shareable, whether it is a full meal), not as the lowest price on the page.

### Confidence
**High** on the existence and strength of the veg-first norm; **Medium** on the exact vegetarian share (survey vs consumption measures disagree by ~2x); **Medium** on the "value through abundance" framing (operator opinion plus platform data, not a controlled study).

### Limitations
Pew's figure is self-reported identity, not diet, and is now ~6 years old. Delivery-platform and QSR value data skew urban and online. No source found that measures how Indian diners *actually scan* a menu (see §9).

## 2. Standard categories and naming conventions

### Key findings
- A conventional full-service Indian menu runs roughly: **Starters/Appetisers → Tandoori/Kebabs → Soups → Veg Main Course → Non-Veg Main Course → Biryani/Rice → Breads → (Indo-Chinese) → Thali/Combos → Desserts → Beverages.** Regional and street-food blocks (Chaat, South Indian) are inserted as their own sections rather than folded into mains.
- **Guests order by familiar names.** Section names that work are the ones guests already use ("Tandoori", "Breads", "Biryani", "Chinese"); invented names ("Chef's Reverie", "The Beginning") force hunting. Trade guidance is explicit about this.
- **Breads and rice are separate sections, not sides.** In Indian ordering they are courses in their own right and are ordered late, in quantity, by the table.
- **Indo-Chinese is a fixture, not a niche.** Real menus across price points (Canada, Ireland, Vietnam, Bali, Bengaluru) all carry a discrete Indo-Chinese block — Chilli Paneer/Chicken, Manchurian, Hakka Noodles, Fried Rice, Spring Rolls, Momos — usually split veg/non-veg within the block. India's delivery data supports its mass appeal.
- **Spelling inconsistency is a live problem**: the same dish appears as *Dal Makhani / Daal Makhni*, *Chole Bhature / Chhole Bhatoore*. Indian guests search delivery apps by common romanised spellings, so the menu spelling and the app spelling should match.

### Evidence / source summary
- Section order and veg/non-veg split within sections: MenuFest (Apr 2025, vendor blog) — *industry guidance*; Restaurant India (26 Jun 2025) — *trade publication*; Petpooja (Aug 2026) — *POS-vendor blog*.
- "Name sections the way guests name them" and eating-order sequencing: Petpooja menu-card rules (2026) — *vendor guidance, based on their own client base, explicitly not survey data*.
- Indo-Chinese as standard block: observed across ~8 live menus (Royal Indian Canada; Indian Zaika IE; Royal Rasoi VN; Darbar Bali; Biriyani Zone Bengaluru; Signature Spice UK) — *observational sample, not systematic*.
- Biryani's dominance: Swiggy *How India Swiggy'd 2025* — 93M biryani orders, chicken biryani 57.7M, veg dosa 26.2M, idli 11M (breakfast) — *platform data, Jan–Nov 2025*.

### Practical recommendation
Use the seven-to-nine sections guests already expect, in eating order, with breads and rice kept separate. Put biryani in its own section if it is a signature — it is the single highest-volume dish category in the country. Label the Indo-Chinese block plainly ("Indo-Chinese" or "Chinese") rather than merging it into mains. Pick one romanisation per dish and use it identically on the card, the POS and the delivery listings.

### Confidence
**High** on the category set (converges across trade guidance and observed menus). **Medium** on optimal section count — the widely quoted "5–7 sections, max 9 items" is practitioner heuristic, not measured.

### Limitations
Observed menus are a convenience sample, heavily non-Indian-market. No Indian dataset on section-order effects. The "7±2 items per category" rule widely repeated by vendors is a misapplication of Miller (1956); **WEAK EVIDENCE** — do not treat it as a finding.

## 3. Vegetarian / non-vegetarian communication

### Key findings
- **The symbols are legally defined and recently changed shape.** Vegetarian = **green filled circle inside a green-outlined square**. Non-vegetarian = **brown filled triangle inside a brown-outlined square**. The old **brown circle is obsolete**; the triangle was adopted partly so colour-blind diners can distinguish by shape. Egg counts as non-vegetarian.
- **Menu marking is mandatory for essentially all food service establishments, not just chains.** Under Reg. 9(2)(b) of the FSS (Labelling and Display) Regulations 2020, establishments must show the veg/non-veg logo against items on menu cards/boards. Calorie declaration (Reg. 9(1)) is the part limited to central-licence holders or 10+ outlets. Allergen information (Reg. 9(2)(a)) applies alongside the veg logo.
- **Per-item marking is necessary but not sufficient.** Strict vegetarians read *section architecture*, not just symbols: separate veg and non-veg sections, and in many restaurants separate preparation lines, utensils and fryers. Menus that interleave veg and non-veg dishes in one list are read as a warning sign.
- **"Veg" is not one category.** Trade and booking platforms now separate *pure veg* (no meat cooked on premises), *vegetarian-friendly* (shared tandoor/fryer), *Jain* (no onion, garlic, root vegetables) and *vegan* (no dairy/ghee). Jain is the one most often missing from menus and most often requested.
- **Segregation is culturally loaded.** Zomato's 2024 "Pure Veg Mode/Fleet" drew a national argument about caste, purity and exclusion. The operational lesson is that veg/non-veg separation is a genuine customer requirement; the presentation lesson is to keep it factual and unceremonious.

### Evidence / source summary
| Item | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Symbol shapes, colours, minimum sizes by panel area (3/2.5/6 mm up to 100 cm², scaling upward) | FSS (Labelling and Display) Regulations 2020, Reg. 5(4)(a)–(c), consolidated version VIII, 09.09.2025 | **Primary regulation** |
| Veg logo + allergens mandatory on menus; calories only for central licence / 10+ outlets; exemptions (caterers <60 days, free self-serve condiments, special orders) | Same regulations, Reg. 9(1)–(3); FSSAI Guidance Note on menu labelling, 23 Feb 2022 | **Primary regulation + regulator guidance** |
| Brown triangle replaced brown circle; egg = non-veg | Multiple compliance summaries (LexComply 2026; Petpooja 2026; FoodRaksha 2026) agreeing with regulation text | Secondary, consistent |
| ~37% of vegetarians avoid mixed restaurants | Pew via Hindustan Times, Mar 2024 | Survey, secondary |
| Separate chefs, dedicated pots, colour-coded boards, separate Jain ("NONG") section with 8–10 Jain items | Indian Express, 12 Jun 2025 (Mumbai restaurant practice) | Reporting |
| Pure veg vs vegetarian-friendly vs Jain vs vegan definitions | EazyDiner, Sep 2026 | Industry content |
| Zomato Pure Veg Mode and the caste/purity backlash | Al Jazeera, 30 Mar 2024; The Mooknayak, 21 Mar 2024 | Reporting |

### Practical recommendation
Mark **every** item — including everything inside the vegetarian section — with the current FSSAI symbol; do not rely on the section heading alone. Order the card so vegetarian dishes come before non-vegetarian ones, and keep the two blocks cleanly separated rather than interleaved. Add a "Jain / no onion-garlic on request" line if the kitchen can actually do it, and say plainly whether the kitchen is pure veg or shares equipment — over-claiming purity is the one error that loses a repeat customer permanently.

### Confidence
**High** on the regulatory requirement and symbol specification (primary text). **High** on the strength of vegetarian screening behaviour. **Medium** on how far section-level segregation (as opposed to per-item marks) changes choice — inferred from survey strictness and practitioner accounts, not measured.

### Limitations
FSSAI's menu provisions are enforced unevenly; many independent restaurants still print the old brown dot. No published data on how often vegetarians actually reject a menu because of layout rather than symbols. The caste dimension makes this topic socially sensitive — recommendations should stay operational.

## 4. Spice indicators

### Key findings
- **Chilli-icon scales are a Western-facing convention more than an Indian one.** Indian-menu guidance commonly proposes 1–4 chillies (mild/medium/hot/very hot) or text labels plus a footer "all dishes can be adjusted, please ask your server". But practitioners note that in India dishes normally arrive at their intended heat and guests are *warned*, not asked to choose a level.
- **The scale is not standardised anywhere.** One kitchen's "medium" is another's "hot"; icons carry no defined Scoville meaning. Two graded levels (hot vs very hot) already communicates more than a single blanket flame.
- **Spice ≠ flavour is the most common misreading.** Indian cooking's complexity comes from masala, fat and technique; chilli is one component. Menus that mark only heat and never describe flavour teach guests the wrong thing.
- **Where heat genuinely varies by dish, mark it on the dish, not in a legend.** Vindaloo, Chettinad, Andhra and Naga preparations are the usual candidates.

### Evidence / source summary
- Chilli-icon ladder + "spice adjustable" footer: MenuFest (2025) — *vendor blog*; Nishi Enterprise (Jul 2026) — *restaurant-supply blog, US-oriented*.
- "No standardisation between restaurants; icons only comparable within one menu": SeEat (Sep 2026) — *content site*.
- "In India I've never been asked how hot I want it; dishes come as-is": r/IndianFood practitioner comment — *anecdote, weak*.
- Two-tier hot/very-hot grading as a quality signal: Differ (Oct 2026) — *blog observation of one Amsterdam menu*.
- Viral examples of spice-scale confusion (zero → "Indian very spicy" charts; "no refunds if you can't handle it" notices): NDTV Food (Apr 2023), Gulf News (Apr 2023), NDTV (Nov 2023) — *reporting of social-media incidents*.

### Practical recommendation
Use a three-step scale at most (mild / medium / hot), define it once in a footer, and apply it only to dishes where heat genuinely differs — a chilli icon on every second dish is noise. Pair heat with one flavour cue in the description ("tangy", "smoky", "coconut"). If the kitchen can moderate heat, say so once at the foot of the card rather than repeating it per dish.

### Confidence
**Low–Medium.** Practice is well documented; *effect* on comprehension, complaints or satisfaction in Indian restaurants is unmeasured. **DATA MISSING** for India-specific spice-icon comprehension studies.

### Limitations
Nearly all spice-scale "evidence" is US/UK restaurant practice and vendor advice. Indian diners' baseline familiarity with dish names makes icons less necessary here than abroad — an inference, not a finding.

## 5. Regional presentation (North / South / regional speciality)

### Key findings
- **Regional identity is a selling point in 2025–26, and it is growing.** Delivery data shows regional cuisines expanding fast: Pahari ~9x year-on-year, with Malabari, Rajasthani and Malvani roughly doubling. Menus that present regional dishes as named specialities (Hyderabadi dum biryani, Chettinad, Malabar, Kashmiri, Awadhi) are riding real demand.
- **North Indian is the default frame** of the "Indian restaurant" menu: tandoor, paneer, dal makhani, butter chicken, naan, biryani. South Indian appears either as its own restaurant format (dosa/idli/sambar/filter coffee) or as a discrete block inside a multi-cuisine card.
- **Regional blocks work best as coherent sections** (a South Indian block, a tandoor block, a Bengali/coastal block) rather than as scattered one-offs, because they signal a kitchen capability rather than a token dish.
- **Cuisine labels carry expectation about heat, fat and vegetarianism.** A "Chettinad" or "Andhra" label sets a heat expectation; a "Gujarati thali" label sets a vegetarian, sweet-sour expectation. Mislabeling reads as inauthenticity.

### Evidence / source summary
- Regional cuisine growth multiples: Swiggy *How India Swiggy'd 2025* (Jan–Nov 2025 data; reported by Business Standard, Hotelier India, 23 Dec 2025) — *platform data*.
- North/South/East/West/coastal/northeast dish and price survey (thali ₹100–250 typical; masala dosa ₹60–150; butter chicken ₹300–600): TravelDojo (Oct 2026) — *travel guide, indicative only*.
- South Indian as separate format vs block: IndiaGo Bengaluru guide (2026); observed menus — *observational*.
- Menus moving toward named regional specialities and storytelling: Livemint (Feb 2026) — *industry reporting*.

### Practical recommendation
If the kitchen has one genuine regional strength, give it a named section with three to five dishes and a one-line origin note; do not scatter regional dishes through a generic "mains" list. Keep the North Indian core recognisable and unrenamed. Avoid regional labels the kitchen cannot deliver — the label is the promise.

### Confidence
**Medium.** Demand growth is well evidenced by platform data; the *menu-design* conclusion (blocks beat scatter) is practitioner inference.

### Limitations
Swiggy data is delivery-only, urban-skewed, and self-reported by the platform. No study compares sales of regional dishes presented as blocks vs scattered.

## 6. Family dining, sharing and mixed tables

### Key findings
- **Indian restaurant meals are shared by default.** Dishes arrive in communal vessels; individuals do not "own" a main. A table of six typically orders fewer mains than six and shares breads, rice and curries. "One by two" (half portions for two people) is a normal request.
- **Thali is the structural answer to group dining.** It is a complete, portioned, often unlimited meal; it removes decision burden, handles fussy eaters and children, and reads as unambiguous value. Price bands observed in 2026: ~₹120–300 everyday veg thali, ₹350–700 dedicated thali restaurants, ₹500–1,200 festive/smart-casual, up to ₹1,200–2,500 heritage.
- **Group ordering is growing fast online** — a proxy for how Indian groups actually eat: group-ordering features grew ~841% year-on-year in 2025. Large-format items (family packs, biryani buckets, thalis for 4) exist because of this behaviour.
- **Mixed veg/non-veg families are the norm, and the thali format lets one table satisfy both** (veg thali + non-veg thali side by side). Menus that force a single sharing mode disadvantage exactly these tables.
- **Menus are increasingly expected to serve multiple generations at once** — mild/comfort options for grandparents and children, something new for younger diners.

### Evidence / source summary
- Sharing norm, thali as shared/communal meal: Mashed (Feb 2024) — *food media, cultural description*; Times of India Pune (12 Jan 2025) on "one by two" portions and group sharing — *reporting with diner quotes*.
- Thali price bands and unlimited-refill practice: TravelDojo (2026); The Unbored Club (Oct 2026); travell.cc Ahmedabad thali guide (Mar 2026, ₹300–2,500 range) — *travel/consumer guides, indicative*.
- Group ordering +841% YoY; group bookings +64.1% YoY on Father's Day: Swiggy 2025 report — *platform data*.
- Restaurants being urged to design for multi-generation family tables, family platters and thalis: ETV Bharat (2 Mar 2026) — *reporting/opinion*.
- Thali economics (a la carte equivalent costs 40–60% more than the thali): splitty (2024) — *app blog, illustrative arithmetic, not research*.

### Practical recommendation
Price and present at least one complete-meal option per dietary track (veg thali, non-veg thali, or a clearly bounded "meal for two"), because that is how groups decide. Indicate what a dish is *for* — "serves 2", "goes with breads", "enough for the table" — since sharing means portion expectation matters more than portion size. Keep half/full or half-plate options visible where the kitchen supports them; they are a normal Indian request, not a discount tactic.

### Confidence
**High** that sharing and thali formats matter (converging cultural description, practitioner behaviour, platform group data). **Medium** on price bands (2026 guide-level, city-dependent).

### Limitations
No Indian academic study quantifying sharing behaviour at restaurant tables. Thali prices vary enormously by city and format; treat the bands as orientation only. Group-ordering growth is measured from a low base and reflects app features, not necessarily dine-in behaviour.

## 7. Price sensitivity and how prices are read

### Key findings
- **Indian diners are price-*sensitive* but not price-*only*.** The operative question in 2025–26 is "what do I get for this rupee", with ₹99 and ₹199 functioning as psychological entry points and combos/thalis used to deliver perceived abundance.
- **A large share of demand sits at low ticket sizes.** Roughly a fifth to a third of online delivery orders fall under ₹200. This is delivery, but it calibrates expectations for casual dining too.
- **Price presentation is a live menu-engineering lever, on borrowed evidence.** The most-cited finding is Cornell's 201 diners in a US restaurant: prices without a currency symbol produced ~8% higher spend per cheque. Indian trade guidance has adopted this wholesale ("write 240, not ₹240.00", with "All prices in INR" at the foot), but **no Indian replication exists** — treat as directional.
- **A right-hand price column changes what guests compare.** Vendor guidance is consistent: a price column invites cheapest-first scanning; a run-in price after the description keeps attention on the dish. This is practitioner consensus, not a measured Indian effect.
- **Price disclosure is now a consumer-protection issue, not just etiquette.** CCPA guidelines (4 Jul 2022), upheld by the Delhi High Court (28 Mar 2025), hold that ordering means consenting to *the menu price plus applicable taxes*; a service charge added by default is an unfair trade practice and cannot attract GST. Menus that bury taxes or hint at a compulsory service charge are legally exposed.
- **Round vs charm pricing is positional.** ₹149/₹299 for value items; round numbers (₹350, ₹600) read as premium. Indian trade guidance recommends narrow price bands within a category so guests choose on taste rather than price.

### Evidence / source summary
| Finding | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| ₹99/₹199 entry points; value as abundance; combos and shareables | Sagar Daryani (NRAI President), ET HospitalityWorld | Industry leader opinion |
| 20–30% of delivery orders under ₹200 | Financial Express, 13 Jul 2025 | Industry reporting |
| No currency symbol → ~8% higher spend/cheque (n=201, US restaurant) | Cornell study as reported by Petpooja (2026) and DineOpen (2026) | Peer-reviewed origin, US only, secondhand reporting |
| Price column invites cheapest-first ordering | Petpooja (2026); Restaurant India (2025); CADDAYN (Oct 2026) | Vendor/trade guidance |
| Bare numbers + "All prices in INR" + state GST/service charge treatment | Restaurant India, 26 Jun 2025 | Trade publication |
| Service charge cannot be default; menu price + taxes is the contract | CCPA Guidelines, 4 Jul 2022; Delhi HC, NRAI v. UoI, 28 Mar 2025 | **Primary legal** |
| Charm pricing for value items, round for premium; narrow bands | CADDAYN (Oct 2026); DineOpen (Mar 2026) | Vendor guidance |

### Practical recommendation
Show prices as bare numbers with a single footer line: "All prices in INR. [Taxes as applicable / inclusive of taxes]. Service charge is voluntary." Keep prices in a narrow band within each section instead of a wide spread, and anchor each section with one clearly premium item so mid-priced dishes read as reasonable. Price thalis and combos as visible bundles rather than discounting individual dishes.

### Confidence
**High** that value perception (not lowest price) drives choice, and **High** on the legal disclosure requirements. **Low–Medium** on the rupee-symbol effect in India (US evidence, never replicated here). **Medium** on price-column effects (practitioner consensus only).

### Limitations
The Cornell result is a single US study, 15+ years old, in a different price culture; applying it to an Indian A4 card is an extrapolation. Charm-pricing effects are contested in the wider literature. GST treatment of restaurant bills changed in 2025 — confirm the current rate and inclusive/exclusive convention with the restaurant's accountant before printing.

## 8. Common Indian menu-design mistakes

### Key findings
1. **Overcrowding.** The most consistently named failure. Long menus raise stock waste, slow the kitchen and cause decision paralysis; Indian trade coverage in 2026 describes the industry correcting years of "something for everyone". On an A4 sheet the pressure is acute — the format itself pushes toward 10pt type and no white space.
2. **Missing or inconsistent veg marking.** Either no symbols at all, symbols only on section headings, or the obsolete brown dot. Any of these loses the strict-vegetarian table.
3. **Price-column clutter.** A right-aligned column with dotted leaders turns the card into a price list and suppresses description reading.
4. **A photo on every dish.** Photos help for familiar, descriptively named dishes; for unfamiliar or ambiguously named dishes they can *reduce* evaluation, and a photo that over-promises relative to the plate served damages satisfaction and trust more than no photo.
5. **Inconsistent naming.** Same dish spelled three ways across card, POS and delivery app; translated names that no one orders by ("Royal Cottage Cheese Curry" for shahi paneer).
6. **English/Hindi mismatch.** Menus are printed in English with transliterated dish names; Hindi (Devanagari) is usually a second line or absent. Family and older diners in Hindi-speaking states may read Hindi more comfortably, but there is no research on preference and machine translation of dish names is a known failure mode.
7. **Tiny type and low contrast.** Trade guidance: body text below 10pt fails in a dim dining room; grey-on-cream fails nearly everywhere. Owners approve menus on backlit screens.
8. **Buried compliance lines.** Squeezing symbols and allergen lines in after the design is locked is what ruins an otherwise good page.
9. **Decorative section names.** "Chef's Reverie" instead of "Starters" costs orders.

### Evidence / source summary
- Overcrowding / item cuts / stock waste / decision paralysis: Petpooja (2026); Nishi Enterprise (Mar 2026); Restaurant India (2025); Livemint (Feb 2026) — *industry reporting and vendor guidance*.
- Nine-categories-before-drinks problem in Indian menus: ZillOut (May 2026) — *vendor blog, India-oriented*.
- Photos: Hou, Yang & Sun (2017), *Do pictures help? The effects of pictures and food names on menu evaluations*, Int. J. Hospitality Management — **peer-reviewed**: pictures help for common descriptive names; for ambiguous names they help verbalizers but *hurt* visualizers. Yoo et al. (2021), *Pictures vs. reality*, Int. J. Hospitality Management (3 studies, n=1,105) — **peer-reviewed**: the larger the gap between photo and served food, the lower satisfaction, trust and revisit intention.
- Naming/section naming: Petpooja (2026) — *vendor guidance*.
- Hindi menu conventions (Devanagari dish names, not translations; keep English food words): Menuthere Hindi menu guide (Oct 2026) — *vendor content*; general menu-translation guidance (Menumigo 2026; GetFreeMenu 2025) — *vendor content*.
- Type size and contrast: Petpooja (2026) — *practitioner rule of thumb, no cited study*.

### Practical recommendation
Cut item count until every dish earns its place; keep one or two photos per section at most, and only of dishes the kitchen plates consistently; print veg marks before layout is locked; use bare numbers in a narrow band; use one spelling per dish everywhere; keep section names literal. Print a proof and read it at a corner table in the evening before signing off.

### Confidence
**High** on overcrowding, veg-marking and naming consistency (converging practitioner sources + regulatory basis). **Medium** on photo density (peer-reviewed but non-Indian samples). **Low** on Hindi/English balance — **DATA MISSING**.

### Limitations
Almost all "menu design mistakes" material is vendor content marketing with no measurement behind it. The photo findings come from lab/online experiments with US and Chinese samples, not Indian restaurants.
