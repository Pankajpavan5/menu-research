# Agent 5 Report — Color, Typography & Design Psychology for an Indian Restaurant A4 Menu

Research date: Nov 2026. Scope: research-based design rules only; practical menu decisions included, final layout not designed.

**Legend:** [SCIENCE] = peer-reviewed/empirical support. [CONVENTION] = industry practice, practitioner consensus, no controlled evidence. [WEAK EVIDENCE] / [DATA MISSING] = flagged explicitly.

---

## 1. Color psychology on menus: appetite, premium, trust

### Key findings
- **Color reliably shapes brand personality judgments, not behavior.** [SCIENCE] Labrecque & Milne (2012, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, ~1,500+ citations, 4 studies): hues map onto brand-personality dimensions (red → excitement, blue → competence, etc.), and this depends heavily on saturation/lightness, not hue alone. Labrecque (2020, *Psychology & Marketing*) further warns that most color-marketing research is weakly controlled — lightness and saturation matter as much as hue.
- **"Red/orange increase appetite" is largely a repeated myth when applied to menu design.** [WEAK EVIDENCE] Controlled studies on colored *food cues* show heterogeneous results: a 2020 Frontiers in Psychology placebo-design study found red and blue coloring of food images did **not** have predicted effects on food wanting. The red-appetite claim circulating in restaurant marketing is mostly marketing folklore; red is better supported as an *arousal/excitement* signal (Labrecque & Milne) than as a hunger trigger on paper.
- **Blue as appetite suppressant:** directionally supported but small and situational. [WEAK EVIDENCE] Cho et al. (2015, blue lighting decreased hedonic impression of food appearance but *not* willingness to eat); blue is rare in natural foods, and blue food/drinks have historically failed commercially (Spence has reviewed this). No evidence that blue *backgrounds* on a printed menu suppress ordering.
- **Premium perception:** black (+ gold) → premium/exclusivity is [CONVENTION] broadly, with partial science: a 2022 *International Journal of Gastronomy and Food Science* packaging study found evidence for black's association with premiumness (product-dependent), and Zhou et al. (2026, *Journal of Consumer Research*) found **less-saturated colors enhance perceived luxury status**. Gold/metallics as prestige signals are industry convention.
- **Trust colors (blue, green):** [CONVENTION] mostly; blue → competence/trust has the strongest support via Labrecque & Milne, but blue is awkward in a warm-food Indian context.

### Practical recommendation
- Build the palette around a **deep, low-saturation base** (charcoal/near-black or a muted deep red/green) with **gold or warm accent used sparingly** — desaturation is the science-backed luxury lever, not "more gold."
- Use red as an **excitement/spice accent** (spice-level marks, section accents), not as the assumed appetite engine. It is defensible as brand personality ("exciting"), not as hunger science.
- Avoid blue as a dominant menu color for an Indian restaurant — weak appetite relevance plus off-brand temperature.
- Keep 60–30–10 style proportions as practitioner convention [CONVENTION], not science.

### Confidence
- Brand-personality effect of color: **High**.
- Red = appetite: **Low** (repeated myth; label it so).
- Black/desaturation = premium: **Medium** (some experimental support, product-context dependent).
- Gold = premium: **Low/Medium** (convention; mechanism plausible via association, few controlled tests).

### Limitations
Most color studies use logos/packaging/single stimuli on screens, not printed menus; effects measured are perceptions and preferences, rarely orders or spend. Cultural response to color varies (relevant for an Indian brand).

## 2. Typography readability science (sizes, contrast, aging eyes, caps, line length)

### Key findings
- **Contrast:** [SCIENCE] WCAG 2.2 (W3C, Success Criterion 1.4.3) — minimum 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text (≥18pt / 14pt bold). For **older diners, practitioners recommend targeting ≥7:1** because contrast sensitivity declines with age (practitioner consensus; consistent with aging-vision literature). WCAG itself notes no strict minimum font size, but sizes below ~12pt reduce the population that can read comfortably.
- **Point sizes for menus at arm's length:** [CONVENTION] menu-industry guidance converges on **11–14pt for item names and body**, **prices same size as the item name** (or slightly larger), and below **9pt older guests in dim restaurant lighting struggle**. No formal published standard was found — this is a practitioner floor, not a study. Menu lighting is often low, which raises the effective floor.
- **x-height beats point size:** [SCIENCE-leaning practitioner consensus] perceived text size correlates more with x-height than nominal point size; typefaces with tall x-height and open counters read larger at the same point size. Legibility research (e.g., Tinker's classic work; more recent reviews) shows **legibility differences between well-designed typefaces are small** — x-height, stroke weight, letter spacing, and size dominate.
- **Serif vs sans:** [SCIENCE] the oft-repeated "sans is more readable" is largely debunked for modern settings; controlled comparisons (Tinker onward; recent reviews) find roughly equal legibility between well-made serif and sans faces in print. Choice should be driven by personality/fit, not legibility myth.
- **ALL CAPS:** [CONVENTION + reading research] caps for full item descriptions and long strings slows reading (word shape cues lost); fine for short section headers. Supported directionally by reading research; magnitude not quantified for menus.
- **Line length:** [CONVENTION] 45–75 characters per line is the standard typographic guideline (Bringshurst-era convention); menus rarely violate this except in two-column cramped layouts.

### Practical recommendation
- Item names and descriptions: **11–12pt minimum** (never below 10pt for anything a diner must read; 9pt absolute floor), tall-x-height body face, generous line height (~1.4–1.5×).
- **Contrast ≥7:1** for body text on any tinted/patterned background — critical because Indian menus commonly use dark or patterned stock. If gold text on deep red is used for *body copy*, check it: gold-on-red often fails 4.5:1. Reserve gold for large display sizes only.
- Prices at item-name size, not smaller, not dimmer (the "hide the price" trick is a dark pattern that annoys and is weakly supported anyway).
- Caps only for short section titles; never for descriptions.
- Description column width ~45–60 characters.

### Confidence
- WCAG contrast ratios: **High** (published standard; applicability to print is an extrapolation — WCAG is written for digital).
- ≥7:1 for older readers: **Medium** (practitioner consensus, mechanistically sound).
- Point-size floors: **Medium** (converging industry guidance, no controlled study).
- Serif-vs-sans equivalence: **High**.

### Limitations
Menu-specific readability studies are scarce; WCAG is a web standard applied by analogy to print. Dim restaurant lighting, table gloss/laminate glare, and stock color all shift the effective thresholds; none is well studied.

## 3. "Flavor typography" — crossmodal correspondences (Spence / Velasco)

### Key findings
- **The core result is real and replicated:** [SCIENCE] Velasco, Woods, Hyndman & Spence (2015, *i-Perception*, "The Taste of Typeface", ~250 citations): people consistently match **sweet → rounded typefaces, bitter → angular typefaces**, with salty/sour in between. Roundness/angularity (RA) of letterforms is the key dimension; the same RA mapping governs shapes generally (Spence 2011 tutorial review; Spence & Levitan 2021). Replicated across countries/languages (follow-up studies) and shown to influence taste *expectations* and, in some studies, actual perception ratings of congruently presented food.
- **Scope limits:** [SCIENCE] effects are on expectations and preference/congruence, demonstrated mostly with packaging, fictitious brands, and young consumer panels; one 2021 *Food Quality and Preference* study found type-taste congruence drove congruent choices but **not among young consumers** — moderation exists. Spence's *Gastrophysics* (2017) popularizes these findings but the menu-implementation evidence base is thin.
- **Scribal/round scripts and taste:** roundness→sweetness/creaminess is the robust axis. Angular, spiky, or aggressive display faces prime bitterness/sourness/astringency expectations.

### Practical recommendation
- For an Indian menu (sweet, creamy, savory, and spicy/charred-bitter dishes all present): use **rounded, soft-terminaled faces for sweet/creamy sections** (desserts, kheer, lassi) and **more angular or high-contrast faces only for charred/bitter/intense items** if deliberate. Default body face should be neutral-to-rounded, not spiky.
- Roundness is a *subtle* lever (font choice, corner radius, rounded ornament shapes like paisleys), not a redesign of the typeface system.
- Use as a garnish-level rule with realistic expectations: it shapes first impressions of dishes, not order volume — no menu uses it as its primary sales mechanism.

### Confidence: **Medium** for the roundness→sweetness mapping itself (well-replicated), **Low** for translated menu-layout impact.

### Limitations
Lab stimuli ≠ menus; effect sizes are modest; cultural/generational moderation documented (fails in young consumers in at least one study); no published test of roundness-typography applied to a real printed restaurant menu (DATA MISSING for menus specifically).

## 4. Indian restaurant design aesthetics (motifs, palettes, avoiding cliché)

### Key findings
- **Everything in this section is [CONVENTION] / industry practice — no controlled research exists on Indian restaurant aesthetics** (DATA MISSING).
- Documented conventions from template markets and design studios (DesignCrowd, MustHaveMenus, Vecteezy, design agency portfolios, Mint Lounge's reporting on Indian menu design evolution): **paisley (ambi/boteh) and mandala motifs**, **Mughal/jaali arch shapes**, Mughal-miniature borders, and a **deep spice palette** — deep reds (chili/tandoor), maroons, saffron/turmeric golds, warm greens (mint/cardamom), on cream or charcoal grounds. Gold accents + arch-framed layouts + gold set text is a recurring formula (e.g., desi menu templates literally place address/phone "set in gold" under an arch).
- **Category-mark conventions specific to Indian menus:** veg (green square-dot), non-veg (red/brown), Jain/vegan marks, spice-level indicators (chilies) — these are functional conventions diners expect and actively use.
- **Cliché avoidance (practitioner consensus):** the failure mode is clip-art Taj Mahals, naive elephant/cobra clip art, over-saturated orange gradients, and every-motif-at-once layouts. The newer Indian premium wave (per Mint Lounge reporting on Indian F&B design) moves toward restrained: one motif system, muted spice tones, more whitespace, storytelling copy — closer to fine-dining minimalism with Indian accents.

### Practical recommendation
- Pick **one motif family** (paisley OR mandala OR jaali arch) and use it at low visual weight: as a corner ornament, divider, or watermark — not background wallpaper. Repeat it systematically.
- Palette: deep muted base (deep maroon/brown or deep green or charcoal) + cream/off-white paper + **saffron-gold accent**. One accent, not the whole spice rack.
- Include the functional Indian marks (veg/non-veg/Jain, spice levels) using conventional colors — these are usability features first, decoration second.
- For premium positioning: desaturate the reds, add whitespace, thin the gold; for casual: higher saturation, filled color blocks, chunkier display type.

### Confidence: **High** that these are the conventions (observable across dozens of examples); the conventions themselves are **not evidence-based** — effectiveness is assumed.

### Limitations
Source base is template galleries and design blogs, i.e., survivorship of commercial styles, not tested preference or sales data. No research on which Indian motifs drive perception (DATA MISSING). Regional nuance (North Indian Mughlai vs South Indian vs Indo-Chinese branding) not covered.

## 5. Font pairing rules for menus (display + body, hierarchy)

### Key findings
- **Practitioner consensus rules, not studies:** [CONVENTION] (NN/g, Adobe design, Interaction Design Foundation, Canva/Figma guides):
  1. Contrast the two fonts in one big axis (serif+sans, weight, proportion, or classification) — fonts that differ *slightly* clash; fonts that differ clearly pair.
  2. A serif/display face for headers + a clean sans or text serif for body is the most reliable pattern.
  3. **Two typefaces maximum** on a menu (display + body); a third only for prices/labels if truly needed.
  4. Family-first: prefer a single superfamily (e.g., a serif with matching sans) before pairing two different foundries.
  5. Never pair two scripts/decoratives; a script or decorative face gets exactly one job (logo/dish-of-the-day), never body text.
- Hierarchy treatment on menus [CONVENTION]: item name (bold, larger), leader dots or flush-right price, description in lighter/smaller text, section headers clearly dominant (small caps / letterspaced / rule lines). Letterspaced caps for section headers are a standard premium cue.

### Practical recommendation
- **Display:** a humanist serif with sharp personality (or a high-contrast serif) for the restaurant name + section headers; **body:** a tall-x-height humanist sans or workhorse text serif at 11–12pt. Concretely: e.g., a "Cormorant/Playfair-class" display + "Lato/Source Sans-class" body, or a single superfamily (Freight, Alegreya Sans pairing).
- Section headers: letterspaced small caps, 14–18pt, with a thin rule or small motif divider.
- Item name bold at 12pt; description regular at 10.5–11pt, lighter color allowed only if it still clears 7:1 against the background.
- One decorative/script accent maximum — e.g., for the restaurant name only.

### Confidence: **Medium-High** for the pairing rules as reliable craft (consistent across many expert sources); **Low** as "science" — none of it is experimental.

### Limitations
Pure practitioner wisdom; no A/B or controlled evidence on pairing rules, and taste varies. Rules can conflict with flavor-typography choices if taken rigidly.

## 6. Premium vs casual visual cues

### Key findings
- **Premium cues** (mix of [SCIENCE] and [CONVENTION]):
  - [SCIENCE] **Lower color saturation** signals luxury (Zhou et al. 2026, JCR); **black** signals premium (2022 packaging study); minimal layouts with a single accent.
  - [CONVENTION] generous whitespace, fewer items per page, thin rules, letterspaced caps, uncoated/heavy paper stock, no price "leaders," discreet price placement, serif display type, understated photography or none.
- **Casual cues** [CONVENTION]: saturated warm color blocks, rounded/bolder type, photos of dishes, playful icons, price leaders/dotted lines, denser layout, chip-style spice/veg badges.
- Menu-engineering evidence (Cornell Food & Brand Lab; Wansink et al.): [SCIENCE] **descriptive labels raised sales ~27–28%** and tastiness ratings (same recipes) — wording/copy is a better-supported sales lever than any color or font choice. Also [SCIENCE]: price presentation formats (e.g., removing currency symbols) showed *no significant* effect on spending in an upscale restaurant test — the famous "$9.95 vs 9.95" claims are shakier than marketed.

### Practical recommendation
- Premium Indian: cream/charcoal stock, desaturated deep green or maroon, gold hairline accents, serif display + letterspaced small-cap section heads, one motif used quietly, no dish photos, 40–60 items max with whitespace.
- Casual Indian: saturated warm blocks, veg/spice badges as colorful chips, rounded display face, denser two-column list, dish photos for 2–3 hero items.
- Invest effort in **descriptive dish naming** (region, technique, story) before perfecting any color — it has the strongest evidence of increasing sales.

### Confidence: **Medium** overall; the descriptive-labels finding **High** (replicated by Cornell team, though by a lab with later replication controversies — treat magnitudes as approximate).

### Limitations
Cornell Food & Brand Lab work (Wansink) has known reproducibility issues; effect sizes may be inflated. Premium/casual cue lists are practitioner taxonomies, not controlled contrasts.

## 7. Master recommendation snapshot

- **Palette:** cream or charcoal ground; deep maroon *or* deep green base (desaturated); saffron-gold accent ≤10% of area; chili-red only as functional spice marking + small accents. Contrast ≥7:1 for all body text; gold reserved for display sizes.
- **Type system (2 faces):** serif/humanist display with soft-to-sharp terminals chosen per section personality (rounded faces for sweets/creamy items per Spence); tall-x-height body at 11–12pt, 1.4–1.5 line height, ~45–60 char lines. No script in body. Caps only for short headers.
- **Hierarchy:** letterspaced small-cap section headers + thin rule; bold item names at 12pt; description regular 10.5–11pt; price at name size, flush right.
- **Indian identity:** one motif family (paisley, mandala, or jaali arch) at low weight; conventional veg/non-veg/Jain marks and spice indicators; no clip-art Taj/cobra/multi-motif wallpaper.
- **Biggest evidence-backed lever overall:** descriptive menu labels — not color or font.
