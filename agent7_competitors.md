# Agent 7 — Competitor & Real-World Menu Analysis

**Brief:** research only, no design. Extract patterns from real, findable menus and published menu-design commentary.
**Date of research:** November 2026. All menus accessed online on that date; prices are as displayed and will drift.
**Evidence labels used throughout:**
- **(a) Documented** — read directly off a real menu I fetched or a direct quotation from a named publication about that menu.
- **(b) Industry commentary** — practitioners, design agencies, trade press, academics writing *about* menus rather than a specific verified menu.
- **(c) Inference** — my reasoning, not directly evidenced. Treated as hypothesis, not finding.

**Up-front honesty about evidence quality.** Three problems recur in this space and are flagged where they appear:
1. Most "menu redesign lifted sales X%" case studies are **agency marketing** with unnamed or anonymised clients. They are labelled (b) and should not be used as statistics.
2. A large amount of 2026-dated blog content on menu design contains **unsourced precise-sounding numbers** ("photos above 30% of a menu → guests pay 15–20% less"). I found no primary source for these. Marked **WEAK EVIDENCE / not used**.
3. The famous "golden triangle" and "menu sweet spot" rules are **contradicted by the one peer-reviewed eye-tracking study I could read in full**. This matters, because most Indian menu-design advice online still repeats them.

## 1. Indian restaurants — what the real menus actually look like

### 1.1 Dishoom (UK) — the strongest single reference case

**Key findings (all (a) unless noted)**

- **The core all-day food menu is short.** Counting the live "All Day" menu page: Small Plates **10 items**, Grills **8**, "Ruby Murray" (curries) **5**, Biryani & Rice **4** = **~27 food items across four sections.** Breads, sides, puddings and drinks live on *separate* menus, not appended to the same page. (Some café-specific PDFs carry 2–3 extra salads in Grills, taking it to ~30.)
- **Every item has one short descriptive line, written in voice, not in ingredient-list style.** e.g. "Gunpowder Potatoes — The seduction is in the tumble. New potatoes are smoky-grilled, broken apart, tossed with butter, crushed aromatic seeds and green herbs." (V)
- **Price sits at the end of the description line, as a bare number with no currency symbol and no trailing zeros padding:** `Keema Pau 11.50`. Same treatment on the PDF menus (`5.50`, `21.00`).
- **Dietary markers are inline, single letters, with a legend at the top of the page once:** `(S) Spicy (V) Suitable for vegetarians (Ve) Suitable for vegans.` They sit immediately after the dish, not in a separate key per item.
- **Each section opens with a 1–2 sentence editorial paragraph before the first dish.** The Grills section opens: "First comes the marinade, then the open-air grill. Picture Bademiya in Colaba…" This is the single most distinctive structural choice on the menu: *prose as section header*.
- **Categories are named in brand language, not textbook language** — curries are "Ruby Murray" (Cockney rhyming slang for curry), rice/biryani are "Biryani & Rice". Note: this is a *brand-voice* choice for a UK audience who already know the brand. **(c)** It is probably a liability for a first-time diner at an unknown local restaurant.
- **Menu discipline is enforced, not aspirational.** In the 2024 overhaul they added 23 dishes/drinks and **removed** others on a strict one-in-one-out basis; a co-founder stated "To put something new on we've got to take something off… there's just not space to do it all of it all of the time." They also reversed one earlier removal (Chilli Cheese Toast) after customer complaint. (a — The Standard / Restaurant Online interviews, Sept 2024.)
- **Regional specials get their own page rather than joining the main menu:** Metro's list of the 2024 additions flags "Methi Lamb Gosht… A special at the Carnaby site only" and "Rara Gosht… special at the Shoreditch site only". (a)

**Source summary:** `dishoom.com/menu/all-day-main/` and the Carnaby variant page (menu text and prices read directly); a Dishoom all-day PDF menu hosted at `uksm.blob.core.windows.net` (structure and price treatment confirmed); The Standard, Restaurant Online, Metro, The Caterer (2024 menu-change coverage); Dishoom Journal interview with executive chef Arun Tilak.

**Transferable principle:** *One menu = one job.* Put the ~25–30 things you actually want to sell on the card, in 4–5 sections, one line of plain-English description each, price as a bare number at the end of that line. Push breads/sides/drinks/desserts to a second surface. Never let the card grow to accommodate a new dish; a new dish means an old one leaves.

**Confidence:** **High** for structure, counts, description style, price treatment (read directly). **Medium** for the commercial reasoning behind the 2024 changes (interviews are the founders' own framing).

**Limitations:** Dishoom is a 20-site group with a famous brand, a design budget and staff who explain the menu. Its category naming would not transfer. It also operates all day (breakfast → late), so it uses *separate menus per daypart*, which a single-shift restaurant cannot copy directly.

### 1.2 Saravana Bhavan — the instructive failure case

**Key findings (a)**

- The **UK PDF menu** (Southall branch) has **four overlapping dosa sections**: "Dosas", "Rava Dosas", "Special Dosa Corner" and "More and More Special Dosas" — plus "More & More & More Special Dosas" in the Singapore listing. "Mysore Masala" appears in more than one of these; "Podi Dosa" and "Podi Masala Dosa" sit in different sections; ghee/no-ghee and masala/no-masala variants are separate line items with separate prices (`Ghee Roast 7.25` / `Ghee Masala 7.75`).
- Descriptions are near-identical templates copied across items: "Thin crispy rice & lentil crepe…" repeated with one word changed. This is the opposite of Dishoom's per-item voice.
- **The same brand's menu length varies enormously by market.** A third-party price listing for Saravanaa Bhavan Singapore states **33 categories and 200+ items**; a Chennai listing reports **57 dishes**; a Chennai (Avadi) listing shows a compact **3-category** structure (All-Day Tiffin / Meals & Lunch / Beverages & Sweets). Same brand, same food, radically different menu sizes.
- The UK PDF does at least **state the inclusion rule once per section** rather than per item: "(All dosa's made with rice and urad dal battered crepe and served with sambar & any 2 varieties of chutneys)". That is a genuinely good, space-saving device.

**Source summary:** `saravanaabhavan.co.uk/wp-content/menu/Southall.pdf` (read directly); menussingapore.com and menweb/restaurant.thefoodxp listings (third-party scrapers — **treat counts as indicative, not authoritative**); hotelfoodmenu.in Avadi listing.

**Transferable principle:** *A modifier is not a dish.* Ghee/non-ghee, masala/non-masala, regular/large are modifiers and belong as one line with a stated upcharge or a size column — not as four separate items. And *state repeated serving rules once at the top of the section*, not in every description.

**Confidence:** **High** that the section duplication and template descriptions exist (read directly). **Low-Medium** on the exact item counts per country (scraper sites, different dates, dine-in vs delivery).

**Limitations:** South Indian tiffin is genuinely combinatorial (a dosa × filling × ghee matrix), so some expansion is legitimate. The brand is also a canteen-format volume operation where a long list may function as a *reassurance* device for its specific clientele — **(c)** inference, not evidenced.

### 1.3 Behrouz Biryani — delivery-menu bloat, and why it does not transfer to a card

**Key findings (a)**

- The Dubai menu runs to roughly a dozen categories: Feasts & Combos (4), Tandoori Kebabs non-veg (12), Tandoori Kebabs veg, Kebab Rolls (5), Biryani & Kebab Thali (3), North Indian Curry Thali (5), North Indian Curries non-veg (6), North Indian Curries veg (7), Egg Biryani (2), Breads, plus delivery-app-only categories ("Biryani with Thums Up Combos (14)", "Metal Handi – Nawabi Biryani (Serves 2) (5)" on Zomato).
- **The same biryani is multiplied three times by size**: e.g. Subz-e-biryani at regular ₹212 / king ₹356 / kilo ₹847; Murgh Makhani Biryani ₹288 / ₹466 / ₹1,102. That is one dish occupying three SKUs across three separate categories.
- **Near-duplicate categories split by cuisine-historical name, not by diner need**: "Classic Biryani", "Lucknowi Biryani", "Hyderabadi Biryani" as three parallel sections of 12–14 items each, where the actual difference is spice level.
- **Every item carries a 15–30 word marketing paragraph**, in delivery-app register: "Succulent boneless chicken simmered in a silken butter-tomato gravy, delicately smoked and enriched with cream for a timeless royal classic." On a phone screen this is scannable; on an A4 card at a table it is a wall.
- Named recommendation category exists at the top ("Behrouz Recommends", 4 items) — a real hierarchy device, but on Zomato it is one of ten categories, so it competes rather than leads.

**Source summary:** order.behrouzbiryani.com/menu (Dubai), Zomato Rohini/Hyderabad/Ludhiana listings, Justdial Delhi menu, noon FOOD Saudi listing, behrouzbiryani.co.uk menu.

**Transferable principle:** *Delivery menus and table cards have opposite economics.* A delivery menu is indexed by search and filtered by cuisine tag; length is discoverability. A card is read linearly by a seated person; length is friction. Do not port a delivery menu onto a card. If you must sell three sizes, use one line with a size notation (₹212 / ₹356 / ₹847) rather than three entries.

**Confidence:** **High** on the observed structure (read directly across multiple listings). **Medium** that this structure is deliberate strategy rather than accumulated drift — no company statement found.

**Limitations:** Behrouz is delivery-first; I found no dine-in card to compare. Its menu register may be a platform requirement as much as a choice.

### 1.4 The Bombay Canteen (Mumbai) — the best documented Indian "modern casual" card

**Key findings (a)**

- Structure is **four named sections with native-language names and English sub-labels**: "CHHOTAS — Small plates, big experiences!", "BADAS — Gather around to divide, share and conquer.", "SWEET DISH — It's a good day to dessert.", plus "CANTEEN MUNCHIES" and "CANTEEN TIFFIN BOX".
- **The section header does work the menu can't do elsewhere**: the sharing instruction ("divide, share and conquer") is stated once per section instead of on every dish.
- **Daypart-restricted sub-menus instead of one long list:** "CANTEEN MUNCHIES — MONDAY-THURSDAY (12 PM–7 PM)" and the Tiffin Box "MONDAY-THURSDAY (12 PM-3:30 PM)". A dish that is only available sometimes is *labelled*, not deleted or silently unavailable.
- **Descriptions are extremely compressed — 3 to 6 words, comma-separated, ingredient-led:** "Eggs Kejriwal — Single fried egg, melted chesse, green chilli chutney", "Prawn Toastie — Green chilli & raw mango hot sauce". No sentences, no adjectives, no provenance stories.
- **The published menu PDF shows no prices at all** (confirmed by a reviewer who checked the official PDF and had to source a 2024 price from a third party). Prices are communicated at the table / on a separate list.
- The 2015 print menu was **set inside a physical format** — an accounting register — i.e. format carried the brand, not decoration.
- Count: roughly **20 Chhotas, 3 Badas, ~9 breads, 1 dessert, ~5 munchies, ~6 tiffin** on the August 2025 "Breaking Borders & Bread" menu.

**Source summary:** the August 2025 menu PDF as reproduced at manuals.plus; Burger Breton's 2026 write-up (which explicitly notes the official PDF has no prices and dates the only price it could source); Indian Express 2015 review (accounting-register format, chintu/chhota/bada naming); Livemint 2026 feature on small plates (chef Hussain Shahzad confirming the chhota/bada structure and ₹600–1,000 small-plate pricing).

**Transferable principle:** *Compress the description to the nouns.* For a local Indian restaurant where the dishes are already known to most guests ("Paneer Butter Masala", "Chicken Biryani"), a 4-word ingredient note beats a 25-word paragraph — it is faster to scan and leaves room for air. And *label availability by daypart in the section header* rather than removing items or letting staff apologise.

**Confidence:** **High** on structure, naming, description compression, daypart labelling, and the no-prices observation. **Medium** on exact item counts (menu version and transcription).

**Limitations:** This is a chef-led, high-price-point Mumbai restaurant whose guests arrive curious and unhurried. Compressed descriptions rely on the diner already knowing the dish. No-prices works because the price point is high and staff-led; it is a bad idea for a value restaurant.

### 1.5 Indian Accent (New York/Delhi) — how premium menus treat price

**Key findings (a)**

- The chef's tasting menu is **one page, 12–14 dish lines, no prices against any dish**. A single price line at the bottom: `chef's tasting menu 135` / `wine pairing 65` — again with **no currency symbol**.
- **Typographic hierarchy is inverted from a normal menu:** dish names are *lowercase and unbolded* ("patrani butter scallop, xo shrimp balchao"), separated from each other by **asterisk rules** (`***`) that mark "choose one" decision points, not decoration.
- The "choose one" instruction is **printed once above the group** ("*** choose one"), not repeated per line.
- The à la carte is not a list of 80 items: it is a **prix-fixe structure** — 3 courses $88, 4 courses $98/102 (2025 reporting) — so the menu presents *paths*, not inventory.
- Also documented: "entire table participation is required for chef's tasting menu" and a gratuity note — operational rules stated plainly at the foot.

**Source summary:** `indianaccent.com/newyork/tasting-menu` and `/new-years-eve-menu` (read directly, including the exact lowercase/asterisk formatting); Robert Rems review Nov 2025 (3-course $88 / 4-course $98); Morel listing ($135 tasting, 3-course $88 / 4-course $102 — note the discrepancy between sources).

**Transferable principle:** *Price is metadata, not content.* One price per section or per person, set small and quiet, lets the food carry the page. At a value restaurant you cannot hide prices — but you can still stop them from being the loudest element on the line.

**Confidence:** **High** on layout and price treatment (read directly). **Low** on current prices (three sources disagree by $10–15 across 2025–26).

**Limitations:** Tasting-menu logic does not transfer to à la carte. Its value here is purely typographic: what happens to a page when price is demoted.

## 2. Modern casual dining — verifiable examples

### 2.1 Chipotle — the "few things, many ways" case

**Key findings**

- (a)/(b) Menu is **four formats** (burrito, bowl, taco, salad) × 5 proteins × rice/beans/salsas/toppings, built from roughly **20–25 core ingredients**; QSR Magazine quotes the company's communications director: "just five proteins and four entrées," "only 135 SKUs in the restaurant," "more than 65,000 different combinations."
- (b) Growth came from **recombining existing components**, not adding SKUs: the 2023 fajita quesadilla and chicken al pastor were built from existing ingredients specifically to "limit additional complexity." CFO's stated test for any new protein: it must bring new customers or increase frequency, otherwise "we have more menu items serving the same customers."
- (b) Industry-wide context: average US quick-service menu size fell from ~80 products (2005) to 85.6 in 2014 per Datassential — the trade press framed this as chains *cutting* menus for speed. (Note the numbers as reported are internally odd; treat direction as the finding, not the digits.)

**Source summary:** QSR Magazine "The Gold Standard" (2015); NRN "Chipotle… operational simplicity" (2023) and "Quick-service restaurants take a bite out of menus" (2015); Harvard D3 case note; Investopedia; a 2025 Substack analysis of modularity.

**Transferable principle:** *Perceived variety comes from recombination, not from line count.* A restaurant with 6 curries, 3 breads, 2 rice and 4 sides already offers hundreds of meals. The card does not need to enumerate them; it needs to make the combination obvious.

**Confidence:** **High** for the structure and the quoted reasoning. **Medium** for the 65,000-combination figure (company claim, repeated uncritically by press).

**Limitations:** Chipotle is counter-service with a visible assembly line; the menu does the work of a display. A table-service card must do that work in print, which is harder.

### 2.2 Nando's — the "core + rotating periphery" model

**Key findings (a)/(b)**

- Core is a **single product family** (flame-grilled chicken) with a **heat-level choice** as the main modifier, plus a small, rotating set of sides and salads.
- The chain **prunes publicly and regularly**. Documented removals: sweet potato wedges + Quinoa/Supergrain/Mediterranean salads (2025, replaced by 3 new salads + 4 new sides); Veggie Burger, Fino Coleslaw, Fino Platter (2017); sweet potato mash, feta, fillet steak wrap (2020). A spokesperson's line, repeated across these: "we are always looking for ways to give our menu a refresh… sometimes less popular items come off."
- Replacements are **near-equivalents** (sweet potato wedges → roasted sweet potato & butternut; Mediterranean salad → House Salad), which keeps the *structure* stable while the items churn.
- **Customer backlash is the documented cost.** The 2017 veggie-burger removal drew a Change.org petition (~2,000 signatures); the 2020 removal drew press coverage and social complaints. This is a real, repeated finding, not a one-off.
- (b) The US menu was rebuilt by a consultancy around **familiar barbecue sides** (mac & cheese, coleslaw) rather than authentic Afro-Portuguese sides — i.e. local palatability was solved in the *sides* section, not by adding more mains.

**Source summary:** Dining and Cooking / The Sun / Tyla coverage of UK menu changes (2017, 2020, 2025 — with item names and prices); The Culinary Edge case page; Daily Mail AU (2024 refresh).

**Transferable principle:** *Prune in public and replace like-for-like.* Removing a weak dish is fine; removing a *category* or a vegetarian staple generates lasting complaint. If you cut, cut an item and put a near-equivalent in its place, so the shape of the menu stays learnable.

**Confidence:** **High** on what was removed and what customers said (multiple outlets, named items). **Medium** on the strategic rationale (company statements are promotional).

**Limitations:** Nando's is a 1,000+ site chain with a marketing calendar; its churn is a brand-maintenance exercise, not a design principle. A small restaurant that removes a regular's favourite loses the customer, not a petition signature.

### 2.3 Panera — the clearest documented case of bloat reversal

**Key findings (a)**

- 2024: Panera announced its "biggest menu transformation in history" — 9 new items, 12 reworked, **and a reported ~19% net menu reduction**. A trade-press-compiled list of discontinued items runs to **~48 entries** including entire categories: flatbread pizzas, grain bowls (Baja/Mediterranean), cold brew line, several sandwiches, several bagels, several pastries.
- The reason given, in the company's own words and in CNN's reporting: the menu "grew unwieldy in recent years as it tried to attract dinner guests"; the fix was "refocusing on… Soups, Salads, Sandwiches and Mac & Cheese" and being "easier for guests to navigate."
- **They then partially reversed**: sesame and blueberry bagels returned "by popular demand" within four months, plus avocado toast and a new grilled cheese. Removing bagels from a bakery-café was a category error, and it was corrected.
- (b) NRN's analysis frames the whole thing as removing *daypart distractions* (dinner pizzas, coffee-shop cold brews) rather than trimming the core.

**Source summary:** Panera press release 29 Feb 2024; CNN Business 29 Feb 2024; NRN 5 Mar 2024 and 10 Apr 2024; pennlive 8 Apr 2024 (full discontinued list); NRN 17 Jul 2024 (bagel reinstatement).

**Transferable principle:** *Cut categories, not just items — but only categories that aren't your identity.* Panera's mistake was removing a core-identity item (bagels at a bakery) while correctly removing off-identity categories (pizza, cold brew). The test is "would a regular notice this as a loss of what we are?", not "does it sell?"

**Confidence:** **High** on the sequence of events and the discontinued list. **Medium** on the "19%" figure (reported as employee-sourced by NRN, not confirmed by the company in the same piece).

**Limitations:** Large-chain supply-chain economics (fewer SKUs = less waste) are a bigger driver than menu readability. The same change at a 40-cover restaurant is a design decision, not an operations one.

## 3. Premium restaurants — short menus, quiet pricing

**Key findings**

- (a) **Indian Accent**: 12–14 line tasting menu, one price at the foot, no per-dish prices, lowercase dish names, asterisk separators, "choose one" stated once per group.
- (a) **Dishoom's PDF card** uses the same price-quieting device at mid-market: bare numbers (`11.50`) with no `£`, positioned at the end of the description line so the eye lands on the dish name first.
- (a) **The Bombay Canteen's published PDF carries no prices at all** — prices exist only in the room.
- (b) A widely-cited academic finding supports demoting price: the 2013 *Journal of Foodservice Business Research* study (202 + 241 respondents) found diners rated **~6 choices per category as "about right" for quick-service** and **7–10 for fine dining**, with both higher and lower rated worse. This is the single most useful quantitative anchor I found, and it is a *perception* study, not a sales study.
- (b) A 2024 meta-analysis of 53 papers (16,522 participants) found menu design's overall effect significant, but **effect on actual purchase marginal**, and effects measured on mock menus much larger than on real menus. Honest read: design changes what people *say* and *feel* far more reliably than what they *buy*.

**Transferable principle:** *Short sections, quiet price, load-bearing first line.* Six to eight items per section is the evidenced comfort zone for a casual restaurant; price set at the same size as the description, not the dish name; the first line of each section carries the section's meaning.

**Confidence:** **High** that these are the observed treatments. **Medium** that they cause sales effects (see meta-analysis caveat).

**Limitations:** All three examples are high-price-point venues where the guest has already decided to spend. Demoting price at a value restaurant can read as evasive — **(c)** inference the design team should test.

## 4. Small / local restaurants with effective simple menus

**DATA MISSING / WEAK EVIDENCE.**

I could not verify a single small independent Indian restaurant's A4 card online. Reasons observed during the search:
- Indian independents in India overwhelmingly do not publish menus; what is online is **aggregator data** (Zomato/Swiggy/Justdial) which is delivery-formatted, not the dine-in card.
- Menu-aggregator sites (menussomething.com, restaurant.thefoodxp.com, hotelfoodmenu.in, menuweb.menu) republish *scraped* prices with conflicting counts for the same branch on the same date (e.g. Saravana Bhavan Chennai: 57 vs 59 items; Indian Accent tasting: $135 vs $150). **Do not cite these as authoritative counts.**
- The only "small local restaurant" material I found was agency case studies with unverifiable figures — e.g. an Ethiopian restaurant in San Diego whose designer reports "81% increase in monthly revenue" and quotes the owner on sambusa sales; a Pune restaurant (anonymised, "Kothrud, 1,400 sq ft, 87 items → 52, +15.4 pts contribution margin") published by a menu-software blog. **Both are (b) vendor marketing. WEAK EVIDENCE. Not used as findings.**

**What can be said with moderate confidence (b):**
- Practitioner consensus on target length clusters at **15–30 items total** for a focused restaurant, **3–8 per section** (Monk Creatives FAQ; The Saral's cafe sizing guide, which explicitly derives its ranges from the 2013 study above and labels its own numbers as planning heuristics, not standards). These are *commentary*, not measurement.
- The most credible small-venue mechanism I found is **combined with the sourcing note**: a single-page menu forces the owner to decide what the kitchen can execute on its worst night, which is the same constraint Chipotle and Dishoom manage deliberately.

**Transferable principle:** *At small scale, menu length is a kitchen decision before it is a design decision.* The evidenced number (6–8 per section, ~25–30 total) happens to match what one cook can execute consistently.

**Confidence:** **Low.** This section is the weakest in the report and should not be leaned on for the A4 redesign.

## 6. Common failures (each with a real example)

1. **Category duplication.** Four dosa sections at Saravana Bhavan with overlapping items (a). Three parallel biryani sections at Behrouz split by regional name rather than diner need (a). *Effect:* the guest cannot tell whether they are in the right place, and near-identical items compete with each other.
2. **Modifier inflation.** Size variants as separate line items: Subz-e-biryani ₹212/₹356/₹847 across three categories (a); ghee/no-ghee dosa as separate items (a). *Effect:* three visible entries for one decision.
3. **Description as paragraph.** 15–30 word marketing prose per item (Behrouz, a). On a phone, fine; on a card, it turns a 25-item menu into a page of text with no scan path.
4. **Description as template.** "Thin crispy rice & lentil crepe…" repeated 20 times with one word changed (Saravana Bhavan, a). *Effect:* descriptions stop carrying information and start consuming space.
5. **No hierarchy / everything equal.** The most-cited design failure in practitioner writing (b) — flagged independently by 99designs, Monk Creatives, HansCo, Clever Cafe, MenuStack, designwiz. Consensus is strong, evidence is anecdotal.
6. **Dense price columns.** Multiple tier structures (Ori'Zaba's "triple-tier pricing", which the company said caused visible sticker shock at the third tier and was removed — b, vendor-reported). Also the general observation that `$`/`₹` repeated 40 times creates a vertical money stripe the eye can't skip (c).
7. **Photo overload / mixed-quality photos.** Best-evidenced finding here: a controlled study (Hou, Yang & Sun, 2016/17) found pictures **raise** liking, willingness-to-pay and purchase intent for **plainly named** dishes, but **lower** them for **ambiguous names** among visualizers. Practitioner consensus adds: photo *quality* transfers to food expectation, and mixed coverage (some dishes photographed, some not, arbitrarily) is worse than either extreme (b). The claim ">30% photos → 15–20% lower spend" is **WEAK EVIDENCE — no primary source found, do not use.**
8. **Cheesecake Factory, ~250 items across ~20 pages** — the canonical overcrowding case, documented from both directions: diners describe panic, FOMO and ordering regret (a: Vox, Chowhound, uprox, Mashed quoting diners); analysts argue the length is *deliberate veto-proofing* for mixed groups and that the fix is a curated "start here" page, not a cut (b). **The honest conclusion is that menu length is not universally bad — it depends on whether the venue's job is solving a group decision or serving one diner's dinner.** A small local Indian restaurant with a regular clientele is the latter.
9. **Over-decorated type.** Script/distressed display faces for dish names, body copy under 10pt, menus that fail under warm dim light (b: Monk Creatives, designwiz, Clever Cafe — convergent and plausible, no measured data).

**A necessary correction to received wisdom.** The "golden triangle / sweet spot" rule that dominates online menu design advice is **not supported** by the eye-tracking study I read in full (Yang/Ochoa et al., published in *International Journal of Hospitality Management*): on a two-page menu, no area received significantly more fixations than any other; only area 3 (middle) received fewer — a "sour spot," not a sweet one. Diners read a menu "like two facing book pages of text," averaging ~9.2 category fixations before ordering. The authors also state that no design tactic has been empirically shown to increase sales. **Implication for the A4 redesign: stop designing around a mythical triangle; design for linear, book-like reading — top-left to bottom-right, with clear section starts.**

## 7. What transfers to a small Indian restaurant — and what does not

### Transfers (with the reasoning)

| Pattern | Why it transfers | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| ~25–30 items, 4–6 sections, 6–8 per section | Matches the evidenced comfort zone and one cook's realistic execution | High (structure) / Medium (number) |
| Price as a bare number at the end of the description line, one size, right-aligned consistently | Cosmetic-only change, zero risk, demonstrated across three price tiers of real menu | High |
| One short line per dish, nouns over adjectives | Cheaper to write, faster to scan, and most Indian dishes don't need explaining to the local clientele | High |
| Section header carries the rule once ("served with raita & salad", "choose any two", "to share") | Saves 20 lines of repetition; directly observed on three menus | High |
| Inline `(V)`/`(S)` markers + single legend line | Standard, expected, low cost | High |
| One recommendation block of 3–5 dishes, placed first | Observed at Behrouz, Dishoom, and referenced in every practitioner piece | High (pattern) / Low (that it lifts sales) |
| Cut a dish to add a dish | Prevents slow accretion; the failure mode is accumulation, not a single bad decision | High |
| Split the card into two surfaces (food card + drinks/desserts/breads strip or second side) | Directly observed at Dishoom, Indian Accent, Bombay Canteen | High |
| Label daypart-only items in the header | Prevents "sorry, not available" and prevents a bloated all-day list | Medium-High |
| Photos: either none, or a small deliberate set of the signatures — nothing in between | The best-evidenced photo finding; mixed coverage is the failure | Medium-High |

### Does NOT transfer

| Pattern | Why not |
|---|---|
| **Brand-coded section names** ("Ruby Murray", "Chhota/Bada") | Requires an established brand and a diner who already knows the code. At an unknown local restaurant this adds a decoding step for the exact customer who most needs the menu. |
| **No prices on the card** (Bombay Canteen) | Works only when staff carry the price and the guest expects a high, staff-led spend. At a value restaurant it reads as evasive and slows ordering. |
| **Tasting-menu / prix-fixe structure** (Indian Accent) | Only the *typographic* lesson (quiet price, lowercase names, asterisk rules) transfers. The format does not. |
| **250-item "veto-proofing"** (Cheesecake Factory) | It is a solution to a group-decision problem at a destination chain. A local restaurant's job is a good dinner for the people already there. |
| **Delivery-menu length and long descriptions** (Behrouz) | Opposite reading mode; also indexed by app search, which the card is not. |
| **Rotating seasonal churn** (Nando's, Panera) | Needs a supply chain and a marketing calendar. A small kitchen cannot reprint and retrain four times a year. Replace rarely, replace like-for-like. |
| **Modular "build-your-own"** (Chipotle) | Needs a visible assembly line and counter ordering. On paper it becomes a matrix the guest must solve. |
| **Vendor case-study uplift numbers** (all "menu redesign = +X%") | Unverifiable, mostly unnamed clients, and contradicted in magnitude by the 2024 meta-analysis (marginal effect on actual purchase). |
