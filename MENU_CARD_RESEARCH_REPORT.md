# MENU CARD RESEARCH REPORT
### Consolidated evidence base for redesigning an old menu card into a simple, attractive, psychologically effective A4 menu for a typical Indian restaurant

*November 2026 · Consolidates seven research streams: consumer psychology, psychographics, menu engineering & pricing, visual layout, colour & typography, Indian cultural/regulatory context, competitor analysis. Research only — rules and structure, not a visual design.*

**Evidence labels:** **[SCIENCE]** peer-reviewed/replicated · **[FIELD]** single site · **[LAB]** controlled experiment · **[INDUSTRY]** trade assertion, not evidence · **[CONVENTION]** practitioner consensus, not evidence · **[WEAK]** thin/contested · **[MISSING]** no usable evidence, stated as a gap.

---

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **Menu design moves attitudes far more reliably than orders.** Meta-analysis of 53 experiments (n = 16,522): large effect on attention, moderate on intention, only **marginal on actual purchase** (overall 0.39, CI 0.34–0.45) (Ip & Chark, 2023). *High.* **A clarity project, not a revenue lever.**
- **Diners shortlist, then choose** — filter on one goal (taste, price, familiarity, dietary constraint), then compare only inside that set (Greene et al., 2024; Simon 1955). *Medium.* **Lead with each dish's benefit in its first 3–6 words.**
- **Attention follows relevance, not coordinates.** The one peer-reviewed eye-tracking study found **no sweet spot**; diners read "like a book", and the only low-attention region was filler (Yang, S. S., 2012, *IJHM* 31(3), 1021–1029, N = 25). **The golden triangle is a myth.** *High.* **Cut filler; mark what you want read.**
- **Serial position is the reliable placement lever.** First and last items in a category list were up to **2×** as popular as the same item mid-list (~55% vs 45%) (Dayan & Bar-Hillel, 2011). *Medium-High.* **Hero dishes first and last.**
- **"Most popular" works; "chef's choice" does not.** Ranking the five most popular dishes raised demand **13–20%** (Cai, Chen & Fang, 2009, *AER*); a later field test confirmed only "most popular", and only for guests **without local knowledge** (Bookwala et al., 2022). *High.* **One honest tag per section.**
- **Descriptions sell; health language doesn't.** Over 46 days and 27,933 diners, "indulgent" labels drew **+25%** vs basic, **+41%** vs "healthy-restrictive", **+35%** vs "healthy-positive" (Turnwald, Boles & Crum, 2017, *JAMA Internal Medicine*); healthy copy across 26 chains was *less* appealing (Turnwald et al., 2017, *Health Psychology*). *High.* **Sensory copy, not virtuous copy.**
- **Long menus don't paralyse diners.** Meta-analytic overload effect ≈ **zero** (D = 0.02, CI −0.09 to 0.12; N = 5,036) (Scheibehenne et al., 2010), appearing only under four moderators (Chernev et al., 2015). In India **choice deprivation** dominates instead (Reutskaja et al., 2022). *High.* **Cut for space and kitchen, not psychology.**
- **The compromise effect is the best field-backed pricing lever.** Middle options gained ~**5% share** across **88,000+ real choices over seven years**, **pronounced in groups but null for solo diners** (Pinger et al., 2016; meta: Neumann et al., 2016). *High / Medium.* **Three comparable tiers per category; make the middle one the target.**
- **Format beats typography.** Dropping the currency symbol raised spend ~**8%** in one 201-diner US study (Yang, Kimes & Sessarego, 2009) — best-supported formatting change available, but single-site, unreplicated, no ₹ analogue. *Medium.* **Bare numerals, one system, no `.00`, no leaders.**
- **One hard legal requirement, one hard cultural fact.** FSSAI requires the **veg/non-veg symbol on every menu item** for essentially all food establishments (Reg. 9(2)(b)); only ~37% of Indian vegetarians would eat where non-veg is served (Pew, 2021). *High.*
- **The strongest lever on a crowded card is space, not adjectives.** At 11–12pt with one-line descriptions an A4 sheet holds roughly **30–36 dishes**; beyond that type drops below 11pt. Read directly: Dishoom ~27 items/4 sections, Bombay Canteen ~44/6; Saravana Bhavan 200+ items across 33 categories is the documented failure case (Agent 7). *High (structure) / Medium (number).*

### Contradictions across the seven streams, and how they were resolved

| Contradiction | Resolution |
|---|---|
| **Golden triangle / "86% of attention"** | **Rejected.** Yang (2012) found no sweet spot; "86%" has no traceable source. Design for linear, book-like reading. *High.* |
| **Currency symbol: +8% (Agents 1,2,3,6) vs "no significant effect" (Agent 5)** | Agent 5's null appears to describe the *same* CIA study, mis-reported; no separate null source was located. **Keep the positive result as one 2009 US study of 201 diners, confidence Medium, and test locally.** |
| **Overload: "long menus paralyse" vs "average ≈ 0"** | **Meta-analyses win** (Agents 1, 2, 4, 7 reject the folklore) — with the Indian condition: overload is rare, choice *deprivation* is the failure mode. |
| **Decoys and anchors** | **Rejected as revenue tactics.** Real in the lab (Huber et al., 1982) but null in realistic stimuli (Frederick et al., 2014) and in online menu trials (Attwood et al., 2020). **One genuine premium item per category is defensible; a deliberately bad dish is not.** |
| **Wansink "descriptive labels lift sales 27%"** | **Numbers removed.** Cornell's Food & Brand Lab was found guilty of academic misconduct; 13 papers retracted. Agent 5 also mis-attributes the replacement evidence to "the Cornell team" — Turnwald and Gavrieli are not Cornell. **Use Turnwald/Gavrieli.** |
| **"Kern study": right-justified prices lift willingness to pay 12%** | **Does not exist** — no peer-reviewed price-placement study by an author named Kern; probably a garbled reference to **Kimes** (currency symbols, not alignment). **Do not repeat.** (Agent 3) |
| **9-endings: "16% more likely to order" vs meta-analytic g = 0.13** | Same study reported differently; the mechanism resolves it — the left-digit effect fires only when the leading digit changes, so **₹299 vs ₹300 works, ₹349 vs ₹350 does not** (Thomas & Morwitz, 2005). | **Attribution errors** | The eye-tracking paper is **Yang, S. S. (2012)** — not "Kim, Shaman" (the brief's guess) nor "Yang/Ochoa" (Agent 7's variant). The currency paper is **Yang, Kimes & Sessarego (2009)** — "Yang, Kimes & Gallo" is a mis-citation. |

## 2. CUSTOMER PSYCHOLOGY — how diners decide

- **Shortlist, don't compare.** The most-used strategies are the most enjoyable dish, the most budget-friendly dish and a familiar dish — often more than one per meal (Greene et al., 2024). *Medium.*
- **Price is a late check, not the first gate.** 66% looked at prices **last** before ordering; 74% viewed the price of the dish they chose (ESRI, 2019). Under a tight budget, diners spent more dwell time on prices and **missed a discount clause** at the foot of the menu (Tomm & Zhao, 2016, n ≈ 190). *Medium-High.*
- **Overload is conditional.** The meta-analytic average is ≈ 0 (Scheibehenne et al., 2010); it appears only when options are hard to compare, preference uncertain, effort scarce, or the goal is "just pick something" (Chernev et al., 2015). In India **choice deprivation** is the complaint (Reutskaja et al., 2022). "Decision fatigue" as willpower depletion is unsupported (Hagger et al., 2016, d = 0.04). *High.*
- **Sequential reading, no sweet spot.** Menus are read top-to-bottom, left page first, ~386 ms mean fixation (Yang, 2012). Sweet/sour spots shift with page orientation and text direction (Kuo et al., 2020) — layout-specific, not anatomical. **Gaze ≠ purchase.** *Medium-High.*
- **Social proof beats chef authority.** True popularity cues work for guests without local knowledge (Cai et al., 2009; Bookwala et al., 2022). Server-delivered scarcity beats printed scarcity (Nazlan et al., 2018). Fabricated scarcity is deceptive and a consumer-protection exposure in India. *High / Low for printed scarcity.*
- **Descriptions.** Appealing sensory wording increases selection (Turnwald et al., 2017; Gavrieli et al., 2022, +43.9% food taken per plate — **English-speaking sites only**); effects on willingness to pay are softer. *High for selection, Medium for price paid, Low for India transfer.*
- **Price is judged relatively,** against the rest of the card, and is a **weak** quality signal in restaurants: a Tel Aviv field experiment manipulated prices without diners' knowledge and found effects "too small to matter" (Heffetz & Shayo, 2009). *High for relativity, Low for folklore.*

## 3. PSYCHOGRAPHIC INSIGHTS — who is reading and why

- **Two value systems run at once.** *Utilitarian* (nourishment, speed, price) and *hedonic* (pleasure, novelty, occasion) independently predict satisfaction (Batra & Ahtola, 1991; Ryu et al., 2010); familiarity flips which leads (Ha & Jang, 2010, n = 607). *High (model) / Medium (India transfer).* **One card must serve both.**
- **Involvement, not demographics, splits the modes** (Josiam et al., 2015) — — and there **service quality (Beta = .253) predicted loyalty more strongly than any menu attribute**. **A menu supports loyalty; it doesn't create it.**
- **Variety seekers and neophobes both need the familiar.** The traits are stable and distinct (Pliner & Hobden, 1992); familiar flavours and preparation lower the barrier to a novel dish (Hwang & Lin, 2010); high-neophobia guests were most likely to choose **nothing at all** in a course (601-person study, 24.5% for starters). *High (traits) / Medium (tactics).* **Anchor each section with 2–3 unmistakable dishes; put novelty in one clause, never in a renamed dish.**
- **Value means "what do I get for this rupee", not "cheapest".** Price sensitivity is a cluster of beliefs (McCall & Bruneau, 2010); Indian framing is explicitly *abundance* at everyday prices (Sagar Daryani, NRAI, 2025–26). *High.* **Show what the money buys: "serves 2", thali composition, piece counts.**
- **Premium guests want verifiable specifics.** Provenance and specificity raise WTP; a food-quality signal at a local independent carried a 40% WTP premium over a chain (Penn State, 2020); an "ancestral" name raised expectations, authenticity and WTP (Valdés-Alarcón et al., 2026, n = 300). Indian guests name consistency, novelty and transparency (KPMG India CX 2025: 30%, 29%, 30%). *Medium-High.*
- **Children decide their own order more than parents think.** Child taste preferences drove **85.9%** of parents' actual item choices (Brindal et al., 2020); **80%** of children in 218 US parent–child pairs ordered without parental involvement; **100%** of children who shared a meal had ordered from the adult menu. Indian data is thin (one descriptive Haryana survey, n = 108). *High on influence, Low on Indian magnitude.* **A 2–4 item family block inside the main card, with mild/spice words.**
- **Groups order for the table.** Real tables chose more variety than simulated tables (Ariely & Levav, 2000; 814 lunch slips, 2,202 diners) — at a cost: satisfaction 7.75 → 6.80, regret 12.4% → 38.4%. At high unanimity people conform instead of diverging (Quester & Steyer, 2009). *High.* **An explicit "For the table" block with serves counts.**

## 4. MENU ENGINEERING — categories, items, prices

- **Do the accounting before the layout.** Classify each dish on popularity × **contribution margin** (price − direct cost), per category, with **70% of *equal share*** as the cut-off (Kasavana & Smith, 1982) — often garbled as "70% of average sales". Cost fully (food + waste + incremental labour + packaging + condiments) and compute margin in ₹, not food-cost % (Kasavana, 2025), on **8–12 weeks** of POS data. *High for the arithmetic, Medium for the prescriptions.*
- **Quadrants are conversation starters, not verdicts.** Star (protect, never discount) · Plowhorse (re-cost before re-pricing) · Puzzle (reposition, rename, describe, train staff) · Dog (remove or replace — unless it is a loss leader, a kids' dish, or the only Jain/vegan option). *Medium.*
- **Items are not independent.** Price changes move demand sideways to siblings; substitution-aware pricing across **48 outlets over two years** produced positive net revenue effects (Noone & Cachia, 2020). **Judge price tests on gross profit and mix, not revenue; change one category per print run.** *High (method); Low on uplift.*
- **Tiering: three comparable tiers per major category.** Middle options are over-chosen (Neumann et al., 2016) and gained ~5% share across 88,000+ real choices, **pronounced in groups, null for solo diners** (Pinger et al., 2016) — which matters because Indian dining is group-led. Comparable means *veg/chicken/prawn* or *half/full*. **Three tiers is a practitioner framework (Mohammed, 2018), not a law.** *High (effect) / Medium (transfer).*
- **Bundles.** A bundle is judged against the sum of its parts and against other bundles; framing matters most when quality is uncertain (Kwon & Jang, 2011). **Thali is India's native, culturally pre-solved bundle — and no peer-reviewed thali pricing study exists.** *Medium for mechanics; Low for thali specifics.* **Two or three bundles maximum; build them from Plowhorses and Puzzles, not Stars.**
- **Price format: one system, card-wide.** Drop the currency symbol; keep numerals unmistakably prices; no decimals (Yang, Kimes & Sessarego, 2009). *Medium.* **Price placement has *no* evidence** — the "Kern/12%" claim does not exist. Use inline bare numerals at the end of the description line where descriptions exist (observed on Dishoom and Indian Accent; Agent 7), align consistently, **no leader dots**, never mix systems. **State shared rules once per section**, not per dish ("served with sambar & any 2 chutneys" — Saravana Bhavan; "choose one" — Indian Accent). *Low on effect, High on consistency.*
- **Price endings.** Left-digit logic fires only when the leading digit changes (Thomas & Morwitz, 2005): **₹299 vs ₹300 works; ₹349 vs ₹350 does not.** A nine-ending target was chosen 49.5% vs 34.2% of pizza orders when neighbours ended in zero, and lost the advantage when every price ended in 9 (Guéguen et al., 2009, n = 1,271). The meta-analytic purchase effect is small (g = 0.13) with **no** effect on perceived quality (Troll et al., 2024); round numbers suit premium positioning (Wadhwa & Zhang, 2015). **No Indian study exists.** *Medium.* **One system: ₹299/₹399/₹499 for value, round for premium, never mixed.**

## 5. VISUAL LAYOUT RULES for an A4 menu

1. **Design for a book.** Diners read top-to-bottom and stop when the relevant section ends (Yang, 2012). *Medium.* Put the highest-value mains and the vegetarian answer inside the **first third** of the read.
2. **Three levels of hierarchy, no more.** Section heading → bold item name → one-line description. *Medium.*
3. **Cut the sour spots.** Yang (2012) found the least-attended region was irrelevant filler — brand blurb, unrequested list. **Kill the restaurant-history paragraph and policy blocks.** *Medium.*
4. **One highlight style only.** Bolding, a single box or a single star creates salience, and salience works anywhere on the page — it doesn't need a corner. **Salience is a budget; if everything pops, nothing does.** Marking a weak item backfires. *Medium.*
5. **Generous whitespace.** Large white space and a "luxurious" typeface each raised perceived luxury and quality in a 2×2×2 study (N = 1,193) (Iseki et al., 2025, *J. Sensory Studies*); less-saturated colours raise perceived luxury status (Zhou et al., 2026, *JCR*). *Medium-High (premium) / Medium (attention).* **Whitespace around the 3–5 dishes you want to sell is the cheapest unclaimed lever on a crowded Indian card.**
6. **Sub-group large categories rather than truncating variety** ("House Classics / Regional Specialities / Breads" inside one category). *Medium.*
7. **6–8 items per section is a perception benchmark, not a law.** Diners rate ~6 choices per category "about right" for quick service and 7–10 for fine dining (Johns, Edwards & Hartwell, 2013, n = 443 — stated preference, not sales). "7±2" is a misapplied Miller (1956); working-memory span was revised to ~4 chunks (Cowan, 2001). *Low-Medium.*
8. **Capacity arithmetic beats aspiration.** Two columns, 12pt names + one-line 11pt descriptions, ~15mm margins, header and compliance footer ⇒ an A4 sheet holds roughly **30–36 dishes**. Beyond that type drops below 11pt — exactly the Indian failure mode (Agent 6: sub-10pt body fails in dim dining rooms; owners approve menus on backlit screens). **If the sellable list won't fit under ~36, print a bi-fold or a second drinks/desserts card rather than shrinking type.**

## 6. COLOUR & TYPOGRAPHY — concrete direction

**Palette**
- **Ground:** cream/off-white paper or charcoal. **Base:** one deep, **desaturated** colour — maroon, deep green or near-black. **Accent:** saffron-gold, sparingly (≤10% of area), **only at display sizes**. **Functional:** conventional green (veg), brown (non-veg), chilli-red for spice marks.
- **Why desaturation, not more gold:** lower saturation signals luxury (Zhou et al., 2026, *JCR*) and a 2022 packaging study found black associated with premiumness; gold-as-premium is **[CONVENTION]**. *Medium / Low-Medium.*
- **Why not "red = appetite":** red raises arousal and indulgence in a lab, but controlled work on red/blue food cues found no predicted effect on wanting (Frontiers in Psychology, 2020). Red is defensible as an **excitement/spice accent** (Labrecque & Milne, 2012, colour → brand personality) — *Low for appetite, High for personality* — not as a hunger trigger. Avoid blue as dominant: weak appetite relevance, off-brand temperature for warm food. *Low-Medium.*
- **Contrast (hard constraints):** WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 minimum **4.5:1** normal text, 3:1 large; **target ≥7:1 for body text** because contrast sensitivity declines with age (practitioner consensus). *High for 4.5:1, Medium for 7:1.* **Gold on deep red frequently fails 4.5:1 — reserve gold for large display type; never gold body copy on red.**
- **Indian motif:** pick **one** family — paisley/ambi, mandala, or jaali arch — at low weight (corner ornament, divider, watermark). No clip-art Taj Mahal, elephants, cobras, orange gradients, or all-motifs-at-once. **[CONVENTION]** only; no controlled research on Indian menu aesthetics exists.

**Type system (two faces maximum)**
- **Display:** humanist serif with personality for the restaurant name and section headers. **Body:** tall-x-height humanist sans or workhorse text serif at **11–12pt**, line height **1.4–1.5×**, measure **~45–60 characters**. *Medium-High for pairing discipline (shared craft rules) / Medium for sizes (industry guidance, no controlled study).*
- **Hierarchy:** restaurant name 24–30pt · section headers 14–18pt letterspaced small caps with a thin rule · **item names bold 12pt** · **descriptions regular 10.5–11pt**, lighter tone only if still ≥7:1 · **price at item-name size**, not smaller, not dimmed · **caps for short headers only, never descriptions** (caps in long strings slows reading).
- **Legibility reality:** x-height matters more than point size; well-made serif and sans faces are roughly equally legible in print (Tinker and modern reviews) — the "sans is more readable" claim is **debunked**; choose on personality. *High.*
- **Never a script or decorative face in body text** — one script at most, for the restaurant name. *High (convention).*

## 7. INDIAN RESTAURANT-SPECIFIC RECOMMENDATIONS

**The veg/non-veg question is the first filter, not a detail.** India is the only major market with a **statutory symbol system** so a diner can screen every dish at a glance. Vegetarianism is large and regionally uneven: **39%** of Indian adults self-identify as vegetarian, **81%** follow some meat restriction, ranging **18% (east) to 57% (west)** (Pew Research Center, 2021, n = 29,999). Only **~37%** of Indian vegetarians would ever eat where non-veg is served (Pew, via Hindustan Times, Mar 2024). *High on the norm, Medium on the share.* **Assume every table has a strict vegetarian, often a Jain diner, and meat-eaters — all reading one card.**

**FSSAI compliance — non-negotiable**
- **Symbols:** vegetarian = **green filled circle in a green-outlined square**; non-vegetarian = **brown filled triangle in a brown-outlined square** (the old brown circle is obsolete; the triangle also aids colour-blind diners). **Egg counts as non-vegetarian.** Minimum symbol sizes scale with menu panel area (Reg. 5(4)(a)–(c)). *(FSS (Labelling and Display) Regulations 2020, consolidated Version VIII, 09.09.2025; FSSAI Guidance Note on Menu Labelling, 23.02.2022.)* **Confidence: High (primary regulation).**
- **Scope:** displaying the logo against items on menu cards is required of **essentially all food-service establishments** (Reg. 9(2)(b)), not just chains. **Calorie declaration (Reg. 9(1)) is limited** to central-licence holders or outlets with 10+ units; allergen information applies alongside (Reg. 9(2)(a)). *High.*
- **Mark every item**, including inside the vegetarian section — headings alone are insufficient. **Keep vegetarian dishes before non-vegetarian and the two blocks cleanly separated, not interleaved.**
- **"Veg" is not one category.** Distinguish *pure veg* (no meat cooked on premises), *vegetarian-friendly* (shared tandoor/fryer), *Jain* (no onion, garlic, root vegetables) and *vegan* (no dairy/ghee). **Jain is the most requested and most often missing.** State plainly whether the kitchen is pure veg or shares equipment — over-claiming purity permanently loses a repeat customer. *Medium (practitioner reports, e.g. Indian Express, Jun 2025).*

**Price disclosure is legal, not etiquette.** Under CCPA guidelines (4 Jul 2022), upheld by the Delhi High Court (28 Mar 2025, *NRAI v. UoI*), ordering means consenting to **menu price plus applicable taxes**; a default service charge is an unfair trade practice and cannot attract GST. **Print once, clearly: "All prices in INR. Taxes as applicable. Service charge is voluntary."** *High (primary legal).* **Confirm current GST inclusive/exclusive treatment with the accountant before printing.**

**Sections guests already expect.** Convergent across trade guidance and observed menus: **Starters/Chaats → Tandoori & Kebabs → Soups → Vegetarian Mains → Non-Vegetarian Mains → Biryani & Rice → Breads → Indo-Chinese → Thali/Combos → Desserts → Beverages.** Breads and rice are **courses in their own right**, ordered late and in quantity, not "sides". **Indo-Chinese is a fixture, not a niche** (chilli paneer/chicken, Manchurian, Hakka noodles, fried rice, momos), normally split veg/non-veg **within** the block. *High on the category set; Medium on section count (the "5–7 sections, max 9 items" figure is **[CONVENTION]**).* **Name sections the way guests name them — "Starters", not "Chef's Reverie" — and use one romanisation per dish everywhere** (*Dal Makhani / Daal Makhni*; card, POS and delivery listing must match, because guests search apps by common spellings). *Medium (trade practice).*

**Regional identity.** Regional cuisines are growing fast on delivery (Pahari ~9× year-on-year — Swiggy *How India Swiggy'd 2025*). If the kitchen has one genuine regional strength, give it a **named section of 3–5 dishes**. **Keep the North Indian core recognisable and unrenamed; never attach a label the kitchen can't deliver — the label is the promise.** *Medium (demand: platform data; "blocks beat scatter": practitioner inference).*

**Sharing, thalis, mixed tables.** Meals are shared by default; "one by two" half portions are a normal request (Times of India, Pune, Jan 2025). Group-ordering features grew **~841% YoY** (Swiggy, 2025) — a proxy for how groups order. *High on sharing norms; Medium on thali price bands (guide-level, city-dependent).* **Price and present at least one complete-meal option per dietary track; show what a dish is *for* ("serves 2", "enough for the table"); keep half/full visible where supported.** Indicate heaviness and spice per dish so a table can diversify deliberately — usually the missing information.

**Spice indicators.** **Three steps at most** (mild / medium / hot), **defined once in a footer**, applied only where heat genuinely varies, always **paired with a flavour cue** ("tangy", "smoky", "coconut"). Scale standardisation across restaurants is nil; chilli icons are a Western-facing convention. *Low-Medium — practice well documented, effect unmeasured.* **Use words as well as icons** ("mild", "hot") — the reader may be a child or a grandparent.

**Photos.** Pictures raise liking, willingness to pay and purchase intent for **plainly named** dishes but can *hurt* among visualisers for ambiguously named ones (Hou, Yang & Sun, 2017); the larger the gap between photo and plate served,. *Medium.* **One or two photos per card at most, on signature dishes the kitchen plates consistently — never a grid.** No Indian print-menu photo study exists.


## 8. RECOMMENDED A4 MENU STRUCTURE (text wireframe)

**Format:** single A4 sheet, portrait, two columns, 15mm margins. **Capacity ~34–36 dishes.** If the sellable list exceeds that, print drinks + desserts on a separate small card rather than shrinking type.
**Global frame:** restaurant name + one-line descriptor, top-left · one signature image only · thin rule · no restaurant-history paragraph · compliance footer.

**Ordering rules**
- **Vegetarian mains sit in the first visible position of column 2** — inside the first third of a sequential read (Yang, 2012) — because the veg question is an Indian table's first filter (Pew, 2021).
- **Hero dishes first and last in their section** (Dayan & Bar-Hillel, 2011).
- **Breads and rice are separate sections**, ordered late in the meal.
- **One recommendation block of 3–5 dishes** at the top of column 1 (Behrouz "Recommends"; Dishoom's anchors).
- **Three comparable tiers** inside a dish family (veg/chicken/prawn; half/full), **middle tier as the intended sale** (Pinger et al., 2016).
- **Thali/combos carry a composition line**, not just a price.
- **Modifiers are not dishes:** ghee/no-ghee, masala/no-masala and sizes are one line with a stated upcharge (Agent 7).
- **No dotted leaders; one price system; a new dish replaces an old one** (Dishoom one-in-one-out).

## 9. DESIGN RULES FOR THE FINAL MENU (checklist a designer or coding agent can follow)

1. **Nothing on the card the kitchen won't serve well and sell** — no decorative anchor dish, no dead item.
2. **Cap at ~36 dishes**, 6–8 sections, 6–8 items each. Cut for space and kitchen sanity, not "psychology".
3. **Classify every dish by popularity × contribution margin first** — per category, 70% of *equal share*, 8–12 weeks of POS data, fully costed including waste and labour.
4. **Read order:** vegetarian mains early, non-veg next, breads/rice late. **Veg and non-veg blocks separate, never interleaved.**
5. **Mark every item** with the FSSAI green square-with-dot (veg) or brown square-with-triangle (non-veg); add a Jain/no-onion-garlic line; state whether the kitchen is pure veg.
6. **Three levels of hierarchy only:** section header (14–18pt letterspaced caps + thin rule) → bold item name (12pt) → one-line description (10.5–11pt). Never all-caps descriptions.
7. **One line of description per item, sensory and ingredient-led.** **No "healthy", "nutritious", "light", "diet".**
8. **Keep familiar dish names intact**; novelty goes in one distinguishing clause ("slow-cooked overnight on charcoal").
9. **Price as a bare numeral at the end of the description line** — no ₹ symbol, no decimals, one system card-wide, aligned consistently, **no leader dots**.
10. **Price endings: one system only** — ₹299/₹399/₹499 for value (leading digit changes), round numbers for premium. Never mixed.
11. **Three comparable tiers in each major dish family**, middle tier as the target, each tier justified by something concrete.
12. **Two or three bundles max** (lunch thali, veg thali, family platter), named by content or occasion, built from low-margin items, with a visible honest saving.
13. **Hero dishes first and last in their section**; **one highlight style only**; at most one honest "most ordered" tag per section, only with current sales data.
14. **Serve two readers per section:** utilitarian (price, "serves 2", composition, tax clarity) and hedonic (one sensory line, an occasion item).
15. **Family and group needs:** a 2–4 item family block inside the main card with mild/spice words; a "For the table" block with serves counts; half/full portions visible where supported.
16. **Spice: three steps max, defined once in the footer, applied only where heat varies, always paired with a flavour cue.**
17. **Typography:** two faces maximum; display serif for name and headers; tall-x-height body at **11–12pt**, line height 1.4–1.5×, measure 45–60 characters; price at item-name size.
18. **Contrast:** body text ≥ **4.5:1**, target **≥7:1**; gold only at display sizes; never gold body copy on red.
19. **Palette:** cream or charcoal ground, one desaturated deep base (maroon/green/charcoal), saffron-gold accent ≤10% of area, red only for spice and small accents. One motif family at low weight.
20. **Photos: none, or one or two on signature dishes the kitchen plates consistently.** Never a grid, never mixed coverage.
21. **State shared rules once per section**, not per dish ("served with raita and salad"; "choose one"; "to share").
22. **No dotted leaders. No golden triangle. No Z-path. No decorative section names. No "109 seconds" claim in the design rationale.**
23. **Footer:** "All prices in INR. Taxes as applicable. Service charge is voluntary." + FSSAI licence number + veg/spice legend; put daypart-only availability in the section header rather than deleting items.
24. **Print a proof and read it at a corner table in the evening, on the actual stock, under restaurant lighting, before signing off.**

## 10. THINGS TO AVOID

**Myths to stop repeating**
- ❌ **Golden triangle / "top-right, 86% of attention"** — no peer-reviewed support; traceable to a 1990s graphic-designer's "focal map", not eye-tracking (Yang, 2012). *High.*
- ❌ **"Right-justified prices raise willingness to pay 12%" (Cornell / "Kern")** — the study does not exist; no journal, year or sample. Probably a garbled reference to Kimes (currency symbols, not alignment). **Never use.**
- ❌ **"Descriptive labels lift sales 27%" (Wansink)** — from a lab with a formal academic-misconduct finding and 13 retractions. **Do not quote the number**; use Turnwald et al. 2017.
- ❌ **"Long menus paralyse diners"** — meta-analytic average ≈ 0; and in India, **choice deprivation**, not overload, is the complaint.
- ❌ **"Dollar signs cost you 8%" as a law** — one 201-diner US study, unreplicated, no ₹ analogue. Test locally; don't promise it.
- ❌ **"Decoys shift 20–30%", "a premium anchor makes everything else look cheap"** — no field evidence; null in online menu trials.
- ❌ **"7 ± 2 items per category"** — a misapplied 1956 memory-span finding; the real benchmark is a perception study (~6 QSR / 7–10 fine dining), not a sales law.
- ❌ **"Red makes guests hungry"** — controlled work on food colour cues found no predicted effect on wanting.
- ❌ **"Guests avoid the cheapest item" / "the second-cheapest wine tops the list"** — no evidence for either.
- ❌ **"Sans-serif is more readable"** (well-made serif and sans are roughly equally legible in print); **"white space improves comprehension by 30%"**; **"photos above 30% of a menu → 15–20% lower spend"**; **"menu engineering lifts profit 10–15%"** — unsourced vendor folklore.

**Design and usability mistakes**
- ❌ Interleaving veg and non-veg dishes, or relying on the section heading instead of per-item FSSAI marks; the obsolete brown circle; a missing FSSAI licence line.
- ❌ A right-aligned price column with dotted leaders that turns the card into a price list.
- ❌ A photo of every dish; mixed partial photo coverage; a photo that over-promises versus the plate served.
- ❌ Description paragraphs (Behrouz Biryani) and template descriptions repeated with one word changed (Saravana Bhavan).
- ❌ Modifier inflation: ghee/no-ghee, masala/no-masala and three sizes of the same biryani as separate line items.
- ❌ Duplicate or near-duplicate sections (four dosa sections; three parallel biryani sections split by regional name rather than diner need).
- ❌ Body type below 11pt; grey-on-cream; gold body copy on deep red; approving the design on a backlit screen.
- ❌ The restaurant-history paragraph and policy blocks (the measured "sour spot").
- ❌ More than one highlight style; highlighting a weak item; labelling everything "chef's special" or "most popular".
- ❌ Fabricated scarcity ("only 3 left") or popularity labels without current sales data — deceptive and a consumer-protection exposure in India.
- ❌ Invented premium adjectives ("exquisite", "royal", "authentic") and invented regional or heritage labels the kitchen cannot deliver.
- ❌ Decorative section names ("Chef's Reverie") instead of the names guests use; shipping a delivery-app menu onto a card; failing to check GST and service-charge disclosure before printing.

## EVIDENCE GAPS (weakest areas)

1. **India-specific behavioural data is nearly absent.** No peer-reviewed Indian dine-in experiment on price presentation, description length, photo density, item counts or thali framing; price format, endings, anchoring and choice findings are **transfers** from US/EU/Chinese data. *(Agents 2, 3, 6.)*
2. **Single-site field studies dominate.** The currency-symbol study (201 diners), the Guéguen pizza study (one French pizzeria) and the Cai popularity study (one Beijing chain, 2009) are existence proofs, not forecasts.
3. **No Indian eye-tracking exists** — the "veg section first" behaviour is asserted by every practitioner and measured nowhere.
4. **Indian price thresholds are unknown**: no data on ₹99 vs ₹100 acceptability or acceptable spend by city tier.
5. **Spice-icon comprehension is unmeasured in India**; all practice evidence is US/UK.
6. **Thali and sharing economics are unstudied**: no measured effect of thali/platter presentation on Indian spend or satisfaction; price bands come from consumer guides, not research.
7. **Price placement (inline vs column) has zero real-order evidence** — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
8. **Hindi/English bilingual preference is untested**, and no verifiable small independent Indian restaurant card was found online (aggregator data is delivery-formatted; agency "redesign = +X%" case studies are unverifiable). No published contribution-margin pattern for Indian dishes by type — "your biryani is a Star" is a hypothesis, not a finding.

**Cheapest ways to close these gaps (Agent 2, §12):** a 4-week A/B on the printed card alternating by week, measuring **item mix and average cover** (not just revenue; one lever per test); log what guests ask servers ("Is it spicy?", "How many pieces?", "Is this enough for two?"); track send-backs on new dishes; and a three-question card at the till rather than a QR survey.
